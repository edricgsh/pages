The model is not the variable. What you feed it is.

Everything from Module 02, reorganised into the order you actually work in: choose the input type, pick the model for the job, direct the camera by name, transfer real performance onto a face, then strip the AI polish back off in the edit.

The big idea

Every decision you don't make, the model makes for you — and it always makes the same one. Undirected camera? A slow sideways drift. Undirected pacing? It slides into slow motion. Undirected grade? Punchy contrast, clean saturation, a perfectly stable frame that reads as a render. The whole module is one move repeated: take each of those decisions back — with a start frame, a reference pack, a named camera move with a start and an end, and a realism pass in post.

01

The input ladder — five levels of control

Lesson 1

People open an AI video tool, see text-to-video, image-to-video, start frame, end frame, references, audio uploads, camera controls, quality settings, and conclude it's more complicated than prompting an image model. It isn't. There is only one axis: how much of the result you hand over to the model. Each rung down the ladder removes freedom from the model and gives it to you.

Text to video
Explore

Words only. The model invents character, product, layout, lighting, everything. Great for concept testing and fast visual exploration — every generation comes back different. The downside is exactly the upside: it may look amazing and still be nothing like what was in your head.

Image to video
Animate

You upload an image and the model animates it. That one image carries the character, the product, the scene, the lighting, the camera angle and the composition. This is why image creation is the most important step in the whole workflow — if the image is bad, the video will be bad. Get the frame right first, then animate.

Start frame
Lock the opening

The first frame of the video, stated explicitly. It locks opening composition, subject, outfit, product, background, lighting, pose, angle and visual style. The prompt then only has to carry what happens.

Start + end frame
Lock the destination

Now the model has an origin and a destination instead of a direction. Use for transitions, transformations, product reveals, before/after, scene changes and controlled camera moves. The two frames must feel connected — if they have nothing to do with each other you get morphing and lost consistency, not magic. The best end frame reads as a natural continuation of the first.

Omni-reference
Build a reference pack

Multiple references at once: character, product, location, style, pose, movement, video, audio, voiceover. Seedance takes up to nine images plus video and audio clips; Kling Omni takes up to seven images you can tag and address by name. The more specific the scene, the more information the model needs — and the more useful the pack becomes.

The beginner mistake nobody corrects

With a start frame uploaded, people re-describe the entire photograph inside the video prompt. Don't. The video model can already see the image. If the start frame is a coffee cup on a wooden table, you never write "there is a coffee cup on a wooden table" — you write steam gently rises from the coffee while the camera slowly pushes in. The image controls the scene; the prompt controls the motion.

A UGC reference pack, concretely

Target: a woman holding a supplement bottle in a bright kitchen, speaking to camera with natural phone realism.

Product

So the bottle stays accurate

Upload the label — and the inside too, if the product opens or has packaging. The model needs to know how it works.

Character

So the person stays the same person

Multiple angles of one face beat one perfect angle. Generate front, three-quarter, profile and back views in an image model first.

Location / style

So the realism holds

Kitchen reference for the setting; a style reference for the phone-camera treatment rather than the cinematic one.

Audio

So the scene is built around the line

Upload your own voice or an ElevenLabs read. The scene gets constructed around what she actually says.

Keep the motion focused. If the character is running, jumping, spinning, changing outfit, picking up a product and talking to camera while the camera flies around the room, you have handed the model six chances to fail. Overcrowded prompts glitch and morph. One main subject movement, one main camera movement, one clear scene — that is usually enough. Seedance is the strongest at motion and it still breaks under this.

Think in shots, not in videos

No current model generates beyond roughly 15 seconds (30 at the outside). A long advert or story attempted in one prompt will fail. Break the piece into shots, generate each with a clear purpose, and assemble them in the edit. Two ways to extend beyond a single clip:

  • Frame chaining — export the final frame of a clip (in CapCut: the three-lines menu → export still frame) and use it as the start frame of the next. Repeat for effectively unlimited length.
  • Multi-shot mode — Kling 3.0 puts several camera cuts inside one generation, so the consistency problem is handled by the model instead of by you.

Seedance 2.0 — the shot-based production toolbytedance

Use when the hero shot has to be right: cinematic action, acting beats, product ads, the premium avatar creative you'd show a client.

start frame → ChatGPT writes a 15s timed sequence → generate → export final frame → chain the next shot

The prompting technique that unlocks it is the timed sequence. Rather than one paragraph, split the 15 seconds into beats so the model knows what happens in each moment instead of guessing the arc. Ask any chat model for "this prompt in a 15-second time-sequence layout" and paste the result straight in.

Also: it is genuinely strong at fighting, physical destruction and emotional acting; add slow motion somewhere for contrast on VFX-heavy shots; and Omni Reference lets you tell it which uploaded image belongs in which beat — the reliable way to build a multi-element ad (cut lime → product hero → man sipping) in a single generation.

Kling 3.0 — multi-shot and character lockkling.ai / promptwise

Use when one generation needs several camera angles, or when a character keeps turning away from camera and coming back as a different person.

Shot 1 setup → Shot 2 camera cut → Shot 3 action/reaction → Shot 4 payoff · 3s minimum per cut · 15s total

Multi-shot is the headline feature: toggle it on and choose Automatic (Kling picks the cuts) or Custom (you decide the timing, the camera change and the action in each shot, from as little as one start frame). Normally the slowest part of production is generating consistent images and stitching them; this removes most of it. More than five shots is possible without the toggle — write one long prompt that labels Shot 1, Shot 2, Shot 3 explicitly.

Omni Reference solves face consistency. Generate the same character from multiple angles in Nano Banana Pro, upload them all as one named element ("Bella"), tag it in the prompt. A side-by-side with and without the element is not subtle — without it the face is simply someone else.

Two working notes: prompt the camera move by name and Kling executes it accurately; and it goes glitchy near the end of a clip, so never put important dialogue in the final seconds.

Gemini Omni — the cost-effective second choicegoogle

Use for in-clip editing at volume, for audio-reactive motion, and as the cheap way to find a winning hook before rebuilding it in Seedance.

Underrated for avatars: the lip-sync is consistently good and the voice sounds markedly less robotic than the competition. It also does something nothing else here does — upload music and video together as references and it will sync the motion in the frame to the track (apartment windows lighting up on the beat).

The cheap-then-expensive pattern. Generate several cheap versions in Gemini Omni or Veo 3.1 fast, find the hook and the direction that works, then recreate only the winner in Seedance. You pay the premium once, on the shot that earned it.
03

Lip-sync — the same character, the same script, six models

Lesson 6

AI video can look incredible for half a second and fall apart the moment the person speaks: the mouth lands late, the teeth look wrong, the face drifts, or the character mimes rather than talks. Controlled test — one character, one framing, one script across every model. The finding that matters most: lip-sync does not need a clever prompt. People overcomplicate this enormously. The test prompt was one plain sentence describing the vlog plus the line to be spoken.

1
Seedance 2.0

Clear winner. The face feels alive rather than a still image forced to talk — cleaner mouth movement, expressions actually connected to the sentence being spoken. This is the model for the hero creative and the client-facing cut, budget permitting.

2
Gemini Omni

The surprise of the test, and now ranked above Veo. Consistently good sync, and the standout is how much more natural the voice sounds. Holds up across different lighting and settings on basic prompts. The sensible Seedance backup.

3
Veo 3.1 quality

Marginally better word-sync than the fast tier — but only marginally, and not enough to justify the price gap.

4
Veo 3.1 fast

Slight mouth glitches, voice needs help, 8-second ceiling forces a shorter script. Once music and editing are layered on, perfectly usable — and it is the value pick.

5
Grok Imagine 1.5

Genuinely clean sync, undone by the voice — robotic enough to feel like a dub laid over a face. Fixable in principle by supplying your own audio.

6
Kling 3.0 / Kling Omni

Improved, still not the model to rely on for talking avatars. Starts acceptably, then loses timing entirely past the 10-second mark and turns to mush; the voices sound robotic on both variants. Useful for B-roll and cinematic shots, not the hero talking head.

The 10-second wall. Kling's drift is not random — it is late-clip. Combined with the observation that Kling glitches near the end of any generation, the operating rule is the same either way: front-load the dialogue, end on a visual. Nothing that has to be understood should live in the last two seconds of a Kling clip.
04

Prompt-based video editing — Gemini Omni vs Seedance 2.0

Lesson 4

This is not editing in the CapCut sense — no cutting, trimming, music or titles. It is taking an existing clip and asking the model to change something inside the footage: remove the piano, make the coat blue, put him in a luxury gym, add a shark. Same source clip, same edit prompt, both models, eight tests.

TestWhat it probesResult
Remove the pianoObject removal + rebuilding what was behind itGemini. Seedance didn't fully remove it.
Red coat → blue coatIsolated appearance changeBoth good — but Gemini silently changed a background woman into a man.
Home gym → luxury gymSubject/background separation, lip-sync survivalBoth solid; Gemini's lip-sync slipped slightly.
Spill the coffee and reactInventing new believable motionGemini, by default — Seedance returned safety-policy violations.
Sunny café → rainy dayMood without rewriting the sceneGemini more cinematic; Seedance over-smoothed and over-saturated but stayed more faithful.
Add a shark behind the diversObject insertion that must feel filmedBoth. The single most commercially useful edit here.
Poured drink → waterMoving-object replacement with real physicsBoth. Motion and physics matched.
"Happy birthday" → "worst birthday ever"Text replacementBoth rendered the letters perfectly.
Re-place the camera; switch to POVViewpoint change without regenerating the shotBoth moved the camera the wrong way (right instead of left) yet held outfit, appearance and tattoos. POV worked surprisingly well.

Verdict: close enough that neither wins outright. Gemini takes it on cost — Seedance is at least three times the price, which is unsustainable across many iterations. Choose per edit type, not per brand.

The real failure mode is over-regeneration. A result can look impressive while the model has quietly changed things you never asked for. Always compare against the original, not against the other model's output — check face, hands, lips, clothing, background people, motion drift and camera angle. Complex edits regenerate more; camera-angle edits regenerate most.

Working rules

  • One edit at a time, then inspect. Google's own guidance for Omni pushes an iterative workflow — build on what already works instead of chasing one perfect prompt. Stacking unrelated edits is how clips drift.
  • Judge against the original clip, every time. "Did the requested edit happen" is only half the QA. The other half is "did anything else change".
  • Watch lip-sync after any edit involving a speaker — background replacement in particular knocks it out.
  • Don't judge the first frame. Watch the full motion; drift shows up mid-clip.

The direction of travel: AI video stops being "generate a clip from scratch" and becomes generate → edit → refine → direct the same clip until it matches the idea in your head.

05

Camera movement — 37 moves, 8 groups, one prompt template

Lesson 5 · Lesson 11

Open any feed and watch AI-generated video: the camera drifts slowly sideways, for no reason, over and over. That drift is the model's default answer to a question you never answered. The cure is vocabulary — once you can name the move, you stop asking for "cinematic camera movement" and start requesting a specific thing.

The six-slot prompt template

A camera prompt fails where it leaves a decision to the model. These six slots are the decisions.

Movement: [the move by name, and what it rides on — rails, shoulder, crane, drone]
Start:    [locked frame, or already in motion on frame one]
Speed:    [ease in, build, decelerate / constant / violent / creeping]
Framing:  [what stays stable while everything else changes]
End:      [the final frame and the hold — or an exit still in motion]
Time:     [real time, normal speed, no slow motion — or exactly what slows]

Worked example, the dolly in:

Movement: dolly in toward her face, camera on rails.
Start:    begin from a locked static frame, then ease into the move.
Speed:    smooth push, building momentum, then decelerating.
Framing:  keep camera height and lens direction constant as the distance closes.
End:      settle into a close-up and hold the final frame.
Time:     real time, normal speed, no slow motion.

The four rules

R1
Name both ends

Every movement needs a beginning and an ending. Leave them out and the model chooses — usually badly, starting mid-motion or cutting off before the move resolves. "Slow push-in" names only the pace; it leaves both ends open.

R2
One move per shot

A push-in that becomes an orbit that rises into a crane is more than the model can hold together, and the shot falls apart. Pick one movement and let it do its job.

R3
The bigger the face, the simpler the move

Wide shots forgive almost anything — fly, spin, sweep the room. Once a face fills the frame, keep it gentle: a controlled push, a slow slide, a shift of focus. Full orbits are where faces melt.

R4
Control the timing

Models drift into slow motion on their own, worst on the spectacular moves. If you want normal speed, say it: real time, normal speed, no slow motion. If you want slow motion, say what slows — the whole world, or one thing inside it.

The vocabulary, by group

Thirty-seven moves in eight groups, ordered from what any tripod can do to what no physical camera could survive. Say the name first in the prompt — models recognise it immediately — and design the image so the move makes sense: ask for "tracking over the shoulder" with no character in the scene, or "the camera dives underwater" with no water, and the model gets confused.

Group 1 · The camera stays put · rotation on a fixed point

Static shot — locked, zero drift; the cleanest cut into the next scene Pan — rotate left/right; keep the horizon level, give it a destination Tilt — vertical rotation; name both ends ("from her shoes to her eyes") Whip pan — a pan so fast it smears; hides a cut between two clips

Group 2 · Closer and further · the most-used group

Dolly in — the camera travels; context falls away until only the face is left Dolly out — the world comes back; the reveal, and how scenes end Zoom in / out — camera fixed, lens crops; no parallax, no perspective shift Slow zoom — creeping, almost invisible; the safest move on a face Fast zoom — a sudden discovery, a reality check Crash zoom in — violent snap onto a face; shock, punchline, hard emphasis Crash zoom out — the frame punches open; comedy is in the new context

Group 3 · Travelling past · straight lines that don't aim at the subject

Truck — lateral travel, lens forward; the environment is the subject Slider — the truck at small scale; parallax is the entire point Pedestal — straight up or down, no tilt; framing stays level Push past — brush a foreground object on the way in; the shot gains skin

Group 4 · Around · the circle must earn its ending

Arc — quarter or half turn; the background delivers new information Orbit — the full circle; spectacular, and empty unless something changed Spiral — around and inward reads obsession; around and up reads release

Group 5 · Tracking · the camera takes the character's pace

Follow from behind — about what lies ahead; walking into the unknown Reverse tracking — about the face; the most identity-stable tracking move Side tracking — about the journey; the background carries the speed Low tracking — below the waist, face never shown; introduce and keep the mystery Vehicle tracking — the car sits still, the world tears past; that's what speed looks like Chase shot — tracking at a run; the framing slips and corrects, and that's the panic

Group 6 · The human camera · making generated footage look shot

Handheld — a locked frame observes, a handheld frame attends Reactive snap zoom — event · late turn · fast zoom · overshoot · correction · settle Snorricam — rigidly body-mounted; the face pinned, the world swinging around it

Group 7 · The air

Crane up / down — up: the place swallows them, how films end. Down: how films begin Drone — free flight; the crane owns a scene, the drone owns the map Fly-through — cross a boundary; outside becomes inside with no cut FPV dive — the tilting horizon is the signature; a level FPV is just a fast drone

Group 8 · The impossible · five of six no physical camera can do

Dolly zoom — subject stays the same size, the background stretches Macro exit — start inside the mechanism, travel out until it's part of the world Probe lens — through the keyhole, the strings, the engine Bullet time — the world freezes, the camera doesn't; the freeze must be total Gravity roll — floor to wall to ceiling; needs readable geometry Infinite zoom — world inside world; two per clip maximum, then chain

The one move that grounds everything. The documentary reactive snap zoom is called out as the single strongest trick for making an unreal world feel real: handheld, following fast action, breathing and lagging slightly, then an aggressive snap toward a face with heavy smear and focus slipping for a fraction of a second before it corrects and lands sharp. AI video's default is slow, sterile, perfectly smooth motion — and smoothness reads as a render. Human lateness is the cure.

06

The 30 director frameworks — moves with a reason to move

Lesson 5 · Lesson 11

The catalogue above tells you what a move is. This library is the layer above it: each framework gives the model a reason to move — realisation, scale, threat, geography, emotion, rhythm, reveal. Every entry ships with a shot size, a lens range, a safer version, the risks, and a prompt template with bracketed slots.

F1 · Slow Motivated Push-In50–75mm · MS–CU

Realisation, decision, threat detection, emotional shift. Risk: without a trigger, this is just generic drift.

Begin on [Frame A]. After [trigger], start a slow physical dolly in toward [subject]. Keep the move subtle and motivated, with controlled ease-in, gentle momentum build, and a precise ease-out into [Frame B]. Real-time action, no slow motion.

The whole framework is the word after. The camera begins moving at the exact moment something changes inside the character — she notices the knife missing from the counter — which is what separates a motivated push from the default drift.

F4 · Meaning-Changing Arc / Orbit35–50mm · MW–M

Reveal a threat, shift power, expose new information, reverse the emotion. Risk: if nothing changes during the move, the orbit is decoration.

Arc around [subject] through approximately [90–180] degrees. During the move, reveal a meaningful change in [background/light/power/information]. Keep the path controlled and readable. End on a new angle that changes the interpretation of the scene.

A politician delivering a calm speech; 120° of arc reveals the protesters and the police lights behind him. Same subject, opposite meaning. Use 90–180° rather than a full 360 — full orbits are risky for identity and geometry.

F12 · Foreground Occlusion Transition35–50mm · any size

Hidden transitions, continuity, and — critically for AI — consistency across a cut. Risk: partial occlusion always fails; the cover object must dominate the frame.

Let a close foreground object fully cover the lens, creating complete visual occlusion. Use that occlusion point to transition into [new scene/time/location], then clear the frame to reveal the next shot. Preserve directional continuity if needed.

The cheapest way to join two generations that don't quite match: hide the seam inside a dark coat passing the lens. Its sibling F21 · Time-Layer Pass does the same behind a static object — pass behind a column, and clear it into the same hallway thirty years earlier.

F14 · Reactive Handheld Snap Zoom35–50mm doc style

Documentary presence, chaos, adrenaline — and grounding fully digital action. Risk: continuous shaking ruins it; the delay and the corrective reframe are the entire effect.

Use a reactive handheld documentary camera inside [scene]. The operator follows [subject/event] with slight human delay, briefly loses perfect framing, then performs an abrupt snap zoom toward [target]. Include controlled handheld shake, rapid corrective reframing, brief motion smear during the zoom, subtle focus correction, heavy physical inertia, and a final settle instead of a weightless float.

One clean reaction chain, in order: event → delay → pan → snap zoom → correction → settle.

F19 · Environment-Triggered Camera Reaction24–50mm · W–MW

Explosions, giant footsteps, waves, collisions, turbulence. Risk: if the camera reacts before the force arrives, the physics read as fake instantly.

Stage [environmental event] first. Let the physical force travel through the environment, and only when it reaches the camera should the frame jolt, sway, or recover. Preserve cause and effect: event first, camera reaction second.

Order is the framework. Creature slams its foot down → dust and shockwave travel toward the lens → only then does the frame jolt sideways and recover.

F24 · Reaction-Led Push-In50–75mm · M–CU

Suspense, Spielberg-style reveal logic, emotional prioritisation. Risk: show the object too soon and the reaction loses priority.

Let [character] notice something off screen. Do not reveal the object yet. First, push in toward the character's reaction and let the emotional response become the key information. Reveal the off-screen cause later or in another shot.

The fear on the face is the shot. Whatever caused it can wait — or never arrive at all.

F28 · Motion-Control Timeline Change35–50mm · W–M

Before/after, growth, decay, repeated memory, controlled comparison. Risk: if the path drifts between passes, the whole effect weakens.

Repeat the exact same camera path with motion-control precision while changing [time/character/state of space/era/light/destruction]. The comparison should come from environmental and narrative change, not from a new camera route.

The same hallway tracking shot three times — busy morning, one exhausted worker at sunset, abandoned years later. Identical path makes the time change the subject.

F29 · Static-To-Handheld State Change35–50mm · any size

Escalation, panic, realism, a state shift. Risk: with no trigger it reads as style rather than drama — the handheld must react to the event, not precede it.

Begin the shot in a stable controlled mode such as locked-off or stabilized. After [loss-of-control event], shift the camera into a handheld response with human instability, preserving cause and effect. The state change itself should be the dramatic beat.

Perfectly stabilised shot of a bride at the altar; the groom collapses; the camera loses control at the same instant the scene does.

Scene-prompt structures that keep working

Across Kling and Seedance the same two shapes come up repeatedly. Both exist to stop the model guessing at the arc of a clip.

TIMED SEQUENCE — Seedance, 15s
0–3s   setup
3–7s   action
7–12s  escalation
12–15s final hero moment

MULTI-SHOT — Kling, 3s minimum per cut
Shot 1  setup and subject position
Shot 2  camera cut or movement
Shot 3  action / reaction
Shot 4  payoff or final moment

And the per-shot block that produces the most controlled output — feed the start frame to a chat model and ask for this shape, one block per shot:

Shot:    Tight cinematic close-up on the driver's face inside the helmet.
Details: Sweat on skin, shallow breathing, eyes scanning the track.
         Golden-hour sunlight flaring across the visor.
         Slight handheld micro-movement for realism.
         Background pit lane softly out of focus.
Camera:  85mm lens, shallow depth of field.
Mood:    Calm before the storm, intense concentration.
07

Motion control — performance capture without the suit

Lesson 8

Upload a video of a real person moving plus a character image, and Kling transfers the body performance onto that character. The motion video controls the movement; the character image controls who it looks like. Available on Kling itself, on PromptWise, and on most AI platforms with identical settings.

The mistake that kills most attempts. People upload an avatar image shot at a completely different angle, in a different setting, and the model has to guess how that avatar would look in your environment. You are not just supplying a face — you must supply the same camera angle, the same setting, the same composition.

The framing workflow

  1. Export the first frame of your motion video — screenshot, or CapCut's three-dots → export frame.
  2. Feed that still plus your avatar's base image into Nano Banana Pro, asking for a straight swap: same background, same position, same lighting, this face. Let a chat model write the prompt if you want; add wardrobe changes there.
  3. No avatar yet? Upload the still alone and ask for it to be turned into a random person while keeping the setting identical. Doesn't have to be a woman — men, aliens, mascots, anything.
  4. Upload the video and the new image to Motion Control and generate. Because background and position already match, the result comes out far smoother.

What makes a good reference clip

Use

Clean, single, visible

One person, body clearly visible, stable framing, clear movement, uncluttered background. Simple videos beat chaotic ones. Good lighting is not optional — bad lighting goes glitchy.

Avoid

Hidden, crowded, prop-dependent

Partial occlusion, microphones or bags blocking the body, several people in frame, movements that depend on props the avatar doesn't have. A clip can be great socially and useless as a motion source.

Match

Framing to framing

Full-body dance motion onto a tight face portrait will not work. Match body framing, camera distance and pose between reference and avatar.

Check

Hands, arms, limbs

Compare against the original side by side: did the gesture and timing transfer, did identity hold, are there warped limbs or broken clothing? If it fails, simplify the reference or improve the avatar image.

The monetisation loop

The point is not one impressive generation — it's a repeatable system built on formats that already have demand. Find a proven short-form clip (a dance, a walk, a reaction), download it with a tool like snaptik.app, screenshot frame one, rebuild that frame with your avatar, transfer the motion, post. Audio comes across too. Post twice a day across TikTok, Instagram and YouTube — TikTok performs best, but run all three.

  • Monetise through affiliate links in bio, a product or fan page, view payouts, and brand deals.
  • It works for any video of any human, not just dancing.
  • Build an entire personality and social page around one avatar.
  • Or point it at yourself: record everything in pyjamas and swap the footage to a version of you that's fully ready for camera.

08

Removing the "AI" look — the realism pass

Lesson 7

The counterintuitive finding of the whole module: realistic AI footage is usually not about making the generation sharper. Raw output — Seedance especially — comes out too clean, too contrasty, too saturated, too stable. It looks like a high-end render, and for UGC ads, iPhone-style content and native social video that is exactly what gives it away. Real phone footage has softer detail, flatter colour, imperfect movement and a slight shake.

01
Drop the contrast

The biggest single giveaway. Add an adjustment layer and pull contrast down heavily — you're hunting for the point where it stops looking like a render and starts feeling like normal camera footage. Don't crush it until it's washed out; keep the clip usable.

02
Ease the saturation

Down a little — the keyword is slightly. Take the digital edge off without destroying the colour. If skin looks too glossy, reduce intensity. If the clip starts looking lifeless, you've overshot.

03
Lift the highlights

A small lift softens the image and makes the lighting feel more natural when the shot is too dense or too fake-cinematic. Don't blow out the exposure — subtle camera-footage feel, not broken.

04
Add a hand to the camera

AI clips feel wrong because the camera is too stable. In CapCut, add a shake effect — then dial it way down. Default intensity is absurd. The viewer should feel the camera is handheld and never notice the shake effect. If they notice it, reduce it.

Toggle the effects off mid-playback and the clip snaps back to oversaturated and obviously AI. This is a four-slider fix that rescues footage that would otherwise be unusable.

Realism starts upstream. The pass is the last 20%. The first 80% is the reference image and the prompt — the language that works reads like a camera report, not a mood board: handheld at eye level with very subtle micro-shake, realistic autofocus breathing, slight exposure adjustment as the light shifts, natural daylight only — no studio light, skin texture shows pores and light peach fuzz, asymmetrical smiling, no beauty filters, no artificial smoothing, no cinematic grading. Then, at the end: ultra-realistic smartphone footage, indistinguishable from real influencer UGC, no AI artefacts, no perfect symmetry.

09

CapCut and sound — assembling the thing

Lessons 9, 10

Most of CapCut's panel is noise. The half-dozen controls below are what actually produce a good short-form video; everything else is over-complication. (Its built-in lip-sync, for the record, is bad — don't use it.)

Set-up, in order

  1. Aspect ratio first. 9:16 for TikTok, 16:9 for YouTube. Deciding this after you've edited is the classic wasted afternoon.
  2. Drag clips in rather than using Import. Preview quality (low/high) only affects your machine's load while editing — it has nothing to do with the export.
  3. Ctrl + scroll wheel zooms the timeline. Essential once clips get long.

The controls that matter

Timing

Split, trim, freeze, speed

Split to cut a clip and drop another between the halves. Drag the corners to trim to an exact duration. Freeze a frame for a cinematic hold. Speed up or slow down — skip curve and velocity, they over-complicate.

Layering

Overlays and masks

Drag a clip upward and it becomes an overlay; whatever sits highest takes priority on screen. Rectangle masks make split-screens — top half one clip, bottom half another.

Motion

Animations and transitions

In / Out / Combo animations per clip. For transitions, black fade and mix are the two that look professional; drag one between two clips and set its duration. Effects (e.g. shake) shrunk down and placed on the join work as improvised transitions.

Type

Text and auto-captions

Text → Advanced → Generate transcribes the audio into captions placed on the beat. Edit any word it mishears. "Apply to all" propagates styling to every caption. The pre-styled TikTok fonts are far more legible than plain text.

Export settings — the mistake almost everyone makes. Exporting at 4K/60fps for TikTok causes TikTok to downgrade the video to 720p. Export 1080p at 30fps maximum for TikTok. YouTube doesn't downgrade, so 4K/60 is fine there.

Sound design — do it last

Finish the visual edit first, then lay sound in, because only then do you know where every sound belongs. Voice gives information, music gives emotion, sound effects make the scene feel physical.

  • ElevenLabs voiceover — pick a voice for the job, not for impressiveness (Rachel for neutral American, Adam for a deeper professional read). Up to ~5,000 characters per generation. Punctuation is direction: commas and full stops tell the model where to breathe. Write how someone speaks. Listen before downloading; fix pacing in the script rather than accepting take one.
  • Voice changer — upload a recording, pick a voice, generate. For faceless content, or when you don't like your own voice. (Full cloning is a later phase and isn't needed for most AI video.)
  • Sound effects — set duration to 4–5 seconds; you never need longer. Type the exact sound (ocean waves, footsteps, camera shutter, city ambience, rain, door creak, crowd noise, engine rumble) and it returns four samples. Use sounds that match the action, not sounds that happen to be available.
  • Where to get it — ElevenLabs direct needs its own subscription, but it's bundled inside PromptWise under the Audio tab. Pixabay is free and copyright-cleared for both music and effects.

Layering, which is the whole craft

Immersive short-form video comes from stacking sound effects, not from finding one good one. Import them exactly like video clips — split, trim, speed them up — then mix by distance: a car right in front of camera goes loud, everything else sits underneath. Two more CapCut audio tools worth knowing: separate vocals strips background noise off a spoken clip, and audio pitch shifts a borrowed sound just enough that platforms don't flag it as someone else's.

The cinematic glossary

The terms the models were trained on. Using them is how you stop describing what you want and start naming it.

Camera & composition

Framing — how subjects are arranged within the edges of the screen Blocking — the arrangement of actors and their movement in a scene Dutch angle — tilted camera; unease, disorientation Rack focus — shifting focus mid-shot to move attention Soft focus — slight blur; dreamy or romantic Depth of field — the range of distance that appears sharp Establishing shot — the wide that opens a scene and gives context Wide shot — whole subject in frame; emphasises setting and scale Medium shot — waist up; balances emotion and context Close-up — tight on a face, for emotion Extreme close-up — eyes, hands, fine detail Two-shot — two characters in frame, usually conversation Over-the-shoulder — from behind a character's shoulder Reverse shot — the answering angle in dialogue Eye-level shot — neutral perspective Low angle — looking up; power, dominance High angle — looking down; vulnerability POV shot — simulates a character's vision Insert shot — a close-up of an object, for a detail Cutaway — a brief shot away from the main action

Editing, sound & light

Match cut — seamless transition between two similar visuals or actions Jump cut — abrupt, jarring cut; often a time skip Cross-cutting — alternating between locations to show parallel action Montage — short clips edited to compress time or show development J cut — audio from the next scene starts before its image L cut — audio from the previous scene runs into the next image Diegetic sound — originates inside the world of the film Non-diegetic sound — score or narration the characters can't hear Ambient sound — background noise that sets atmosphere Foley — custom-recorded effects added in post for realism Voiceover — narration over the visuals Overcranking — shoot at a high frame rate → slow motion on playback Undercranking — shoot at a low frame rate → fast motion Chiaroscuro — strong light/dark contrast for mood Practical lighting — real sources visible in frame: lamps, candles Over- / underexposure — too much / too little light on the sensor Colour grading — altering colour for mood and continuity Green screen — chroma-key background replacement Storyboard — the drawn plan of every shot
