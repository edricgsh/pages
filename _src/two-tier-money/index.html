There are two kinds of money, and they never touch.

Banks and central banks aren't sharing one pot. They run two separate kinds of money on two separate levels. Once you can see where the line between them sits, the rest falls out: why banks borrow from each other overnight, why one decision produces dozens of different interest rates, and why a rate rise can make your credit card dearer and a mortgage cheaper at the same time.

The whole thing in four sentences

Banks use one kind of money with each other. You use another kind. They never swap. The central bank sets the price of the first kind — and every rate you will ever be quoted is that price with markups stacked on top.

Banks are the only ones standing on both floors. That is what makes them banks.

01

Two kinds of money

Start here

Say your employer banks with Bank A and you bank with Bank B. On payday, their balance drops by $5,000 and yours goes up by $5,000. That's the whole event. Nothing physically moved anywhere.

But Bank A now owes Bank B $5,000, and that debt is real. It gets settled with a completely different kind of money: the balance Bank A holds in its account at the central bank.

You can't open one of those accounts. Neither can your employer or any company you've heard of. Only banks can. So two kinds of money run alongside each other — the kind banks use between themselves, and the kind everyone else uses — and neither one ever turns into the other.

Where your kind comes from is the surprising part. Banks don't lend out money that savers put in. When a bank approves your mortgage, it types the balance into existence on the spot. The loan and the deposit are created in the same keystroke.

The banks' kind of money works the opposite way round. No amount of lending creates a single unit of it. Only the central bank can make more, and once it has, the banking system can't get rid of it — banks can pass it between themselves, but the total only changes when the central bank changes it.

So what stops banks lending forever? Not how much money they hold. Regulators make them set aside a slice of their own capital against every loan, and when that slice runs out they stop — no matter how cheap money is, and no matter how much sits in their central bank account. Someone creditworthy also has to actually want to borrow.

02

The central bank sets exactly one price

And only one

All day long, payments run in both directions between every pair of banks. By closing time each bank is either short or long. A bank that's short has to find the shortfall before the day ends, and the cheapest place to find it is a bank that ended up long. So they lend to each other overnight.

That overnight market is small, dull, and the most important price in finance — because it's the only one the central bank can set directly. It never has to touch your mortgage. It just has to make holding money upstairs worth a specific amount, and every other rate in the economy has to arrange itself around that.

How it works has quietly changed. Textbooks say the central bank controls how much of the upstairs money exists, keeping it scarce so the price responds. It doesn't really bother anymore. After 2008 it created so much that scarcity stopped being a lever at all. Now it simply announces what that money earns just sitting in the account — and no bank will lend to another for less than it can get by doing nothing.

That's the entire mechanism. One announced number, upstairs. Everything below is other people adding their own markups to it.

03

Two roads out of that one price

Not one ladder

Rates are usually drawn as a single ladder heading downwards — central bank, then banks, then you. That's wrong in a way that matters, because it can't explain the thing people watch most closely.

The path actually splits in two straight away. One road carries today's rate: it reaches anything that floats and resets often. The other carries what people expect the rate to be over the next several years: it reaches anything locked in for a long time.

One decision, two roads, and they can move in opposite directions.

This is what people mean when they say the yield curve has inverted. Short rates are up because the central bank put them up. Long rates are down because the market now thinks those rates will have to be cut in a couple of years, and would rather lock in today's yield before that happens. A single ladder has no way to draw that.

It also explains why the American 30-year mortgage barely flinches on the day of a rate decision. It isn't on that road. It follows long-term government bonds, which move on expectations that were already priced in weeks earlier.

04

What's actually inside any interest rate

Four ingredients

Every rate you'll ever be quoted is made of the same four things. Once you can name them, you don't have to memorise a ladder — you can take apart any rate you run into.

1
The base cost of time

What the central bank's rate is expected to average over the life of the loan. Nobody lends for nothing; this is the floor.

2
Extra for locking it up

Lend for thirty years and you're stuck if that expectation turns out wrong. You get paid for taking that chance.

3
Extra in case they don't pay

The borrower might default. On a mortgage they might also pay you back early, which is its own headache when you'd priced a thirty-year asset.

4
The bank's cut

Staff, branches, systems, and profit. Somebody has to be paid for arranging the whole thing.

Made-up numbers — the shape is the point, not the levels. Notice the bottom bar runs the other way.

That last bar is the one worth staring at. Every other rate is built by adding markups on top of the base. Your savings rate is built by subtracting from it. Same four ingredients, opposite direction — which is a bank's entire business model in one picture.

And the subtraction is generous. Banks typically pass on well under half of a rate rise to savers, and they take their time about it. On the way down they're noticeably quicker. What keeps this honest is your ability to leave: when moving your money somewhere better takes a few taps on a phone, a stingy savings rate stops being free profit and starts being a funding problem. Several American banks discovered exactly that in 2023.

One last thing worth carrying around: none of this happens quickly. A change in the central bank's rate takes roughly a year to eighteen months to fully land on the economy. Which means the people setting it are always steering by a picture of the road they saw a year ago.

05

Singapore does it completely differently

No rate at all

Everything above assumed a central bank that sets an interest rate. Singapore's doesn't. It has no policy rate whatsoever.

Instead, the Monetary Authority of Singapore manages the value of the Singapore dollar against a basket of its trading partners' currencies, keeping it inside a band it deliberately doesn't publish. Tightening means letting the dollar climb faster. Loosening means letting it climb more slowly.

The reasoning is straightforward: Singapore imports nearly everything it consumes, so what the currency is worth hits shop prices far more directly than what borrowing costs would.

But you can't have both. When money can move freely in and out of a country, you can control your currency or your interest rates — not both. Singapore picked the currency, which means it takes whatever interest rates the world hands it. When the US raises rates, Singapore's rates rise too. Nobody in Singapore chose that, and nobody there can stop it.

So SORA — the rate you'll see on a Singapore mortgage — isn't a target being defended by anyone. It's a readout: the average rate banks actually charged each other overnight, published after the fact. Local mortgages sit on top of it, or on a rate the bank sets internally, or fixed for a couple of years.

Every rung of the ladder is there except the top one. Singapore borrows that one from abroad.

06

The names you'll run into

Glossary

None of these are needed to follow the argument above. They're here so the jargon stops being a wall when you meet it somewhere else.

You'll seeIt means
ReservesThe upstairs money. What banks hold at the central bank and settle with each other in. You can't have any.
DepositsThe downstairs money — your bank balance. A promise from the bank, not a stash of anything.
Policy rateThe one price. Called the fed funds target in the US, Bank Rate in the UK.
EFFR · SONIA · SORAWhat banks actually charged each other overnight, on trust alone, averaged and published daily. US, UK and Singapore respectively.
SOFRSame idea, but the overnight loan is backed by government bonds. Most overnight lending works this way now, which is why it replaced LIBOR.
Prime rateIn the US, a convention rather than a calculation: exactly 3 percentage points above the top of the central bank's target. Drives credit cards and home equity lines, and not much else.
Term premiumIngredient 2 — the extra you're paid for locking money up for years rather than overnight.
Credit spreadIngredient 3 — the extra you're paid for the chance the borrower doesn't pay you back.
Deposit betaThe share of a rate rise a bank passes on to savers. Usually well under half.
Quantitative easingThe central bank creating upstairs money to buy long-term bonds. It's an attack on Road 2 — used when Road 1 is already at zero.
S$NEERThe Singapore dollar measured against a weighted basket of trading partners' currencies. Singapore's actual policy lever, in place of a rate.

And the one-line version of everything above, if you only keep one thing: the central bank sets the base cost of borrowing time, and every layer between it and you adds a charge for risk, for waiting, and for doing the work. That's why one decision comes out the other end as dozens of different rates — moving at different speeds, and occasionally in different directions.
