Record the voice and the face once. Deploy them forever.

Module 07 in three lessons: clone your voice in ElevenLabs, put it on a face with HeyGen's photo-to-video avatar, then stop thinking of either as "a video" and start thinking of them as a production line — one clone, redeployed across every format the content calendar needs.

The one idea

A clone is not a video you made once — it's an asset you can recall on demand. Every lesson in this module is building toward the same handoff: stop trading your time for footage, and start trading a script for footage. Once the voice is trained and the face is uploaded, every video after the first is a text edit, not a shoot.

01

Voice cloning — the setup

Lesson 1

Everything downstream — HeyGen avatars, ad variations, course lessons at scale — assumes a voice clone already exists. ElevenLabs is where it gets built: Voices → Create a Voice, then a choice between two cloning modes that trade speed for fidelity.

Instant Voice Clone

Available on any plan, ready in minutes

Feed it a short sample and it returns a usable voice almost immediately. The results are genuinely decent — fine for testing a script or a one-off, not what you build a brand's permanent voice on.

Professional Voice Cloning

Gated to higher plans, slower, the one that lasts

Takes real training time and approval before it lands in your voice library — but if the clone has to sound like you across months of content, this is the mode, not instant.

The training audio checklist

The clone is only as good as what you feed it. Below the plan-tier decision, quality comes down entirely to the recording, not the model:

  • At least 30 minutes of training audio, minimum
  • Clean: no background noise, no music, no echo, no compression
  • Recorded in a quiet room, on a decent microphone
  • Spoken naturally — the register of a YouTube video or an ad, not a stiff read
  • Either record fresh material inside ElevenLabs, or upload past footage of yourself talking — both count as training audio
Name it before you submit it. Once several voices sit in the same library, an unnamed clone becomes a guessing game months later. Name the voice at submission, then send it for training — professional cloning takes real processing and approval time before it shows up as usable.

02

Generation and settings — getting a clean take out

Lesson 1 · Lesson 2

Once a voice is approved, generating from it is the same four steps every time: open Text to Speech, select the cloned voice, paste in a script — written by hand or by ChatGPT — and hit generate. The two things worth getting right before that last click are what the script sounds like out loud, and which voice you're actually reaching for.

Punctuation is direction, not decoration

The model reads commas and full stops as breath marks. A script written as a block of prose comes back flat; a script written the way the line is actually spoken — with the pauses marked — comes back with real cadence. Write for the ear, not the page.

Pick the voice for the job, not for how impressive it sounds

A neutral, general-American read (ElevenLabs' Rachel) covers most explainer and hook work. A deeper, more authoritative read (Adam) suits a professional or instructional tone. Match the register to what the script is doing before reaching for something more distinctive.

Listen before you download. Generation is cheap enough that there's no reason to accept the first take. If the pacing is off, the fix belongs in the script — an added comma, a shorter sentence, a reworded line — not in a settings panel. Each generation covers roughly 5,000 characters; split a longer script across calls rather than forcing it through in one.

The lighter option: Voice Changer

Not every job needs a full clone. Voice Changer takes a recording you already made, applies a different voice on top, and returns the result — useful for faceless content, or simply when you'd rather not hear your own voice on the final cut. Full cloning is the investment you make once you're committed to a consistent on-camera or on-mic identity; for a single video, the changer is faster to reach for.

03

HeyGen avatar creation — the talking headshot

Lesson 2

A voice clone gets you audio. HeyGen's photo-to-video avatar model is what turns a still photo into a talking head that speaks it — and for long-form or talking-headshot content specifically, it's the strongest option available, on one condition: you don't need the character to move. Veo 3.1 will give you movement, but caps out at roughly 8 seconds per generation, which turns a two-minute video into a stitching exercise. If a static, talking figure is enough, HeyGen wins on effort alone.

Photo-to-video, in order

  1. Get a face. Generate a character in Nano Banana Pro (or your image generator of choice), or skip generation entirely and upload a real photo of yourself.
  2. Create → Photo to Video. Top-left corner of HeyGen.
  3. Upload the character image on the left side of the screen — this is the face that will speak.
  4. Upload the audio, or type the script, on the right side. HeyGen accepts audio uploaded directly from ElevenLabs, which is the detail that makes voice consistency across many generations a solved problem instead of a recurring struggle.
  5. Select 1080p and generate. There's no hard limit on script length, unlike the roughly 15-second ceiling on most video-generation models — HeyGen is built for the long-form case.
Save the voice, reuse the voice. HeyGen lets you save voices you use often, so the same ElevenLabs clone — or your own recorded voice sitting on top of an avatar photo of yourself, sat down and framed like a talking head — becomes a one-click selection on every future generation rather than a re-upload.

04

The b-roll cut — editing around the lip-sync

Lesson 2

HeyGen's lip-sync isn't flawless, and the model has no motion to fall back on to hide it — which is exactly why the edit matters more here than on any generated clip with camera movement. The fix isn't a better generation, it's a familiar TV trick: never let the avatar carry the whole video alone.

The cut-back rhythm

When the script mentions a specific thing, cut to a shot of that thing on screen, over the avatar's audio. Hold it briefly, cut back to the avatar for a beat, then return to b-roll. That rhythm — avatar, cutaway, avatar, cutaway — reads as far more natural than a static talking head running the entire length of the video, and it's the same principle a UGC ad made in Veo3 depends on: the viewer wants to see the product, not just the person describing it.

B-roll is also a glitch patch. Any generated clip can come back with a rough few seconds — a warped mouth shape, a flicker. Rather than regenerating the entire take to fix a five-second problem, cover the bad stretch with a b-roll cutaway. The audio keeps running underneath; the viewer never sees the artefact.

None of this is specific to HeyGen. The same cut-back discipline applies to any talking-head generation, and it's the difference between footage that reads as "an AI made this" and footage that reads as produced content — regardless of which model rendered the face.

05

One clone, many jobs — the six deployments

Lesson 3

Everything above produces one asset: a face that can say anything, in your voice, on demand. The shift this lesson makes is refusing to think of that asset as a video — it's a production line. The same clone, redeployed, covers six distinct jobs without a single new recording.

Talking-head contentdaily output

Use for hooks, explanations and authority clips on TikTok, Reels, Shorts and ads — anywhere daily short-form is expected.

The camera day disappears entirely. What used to require setting up a shot now requires a script.

Ad variations without reshootingperformance

Use when a creative is fatiguing and needs a refresh, or when you want to test which version of a message actually converts.

Same message with a different hook, the same script at a different pace, the same ad with several different CTAs — each is a text edit and a regeneration, not a new shoot.

Course lessons at scaleeducation

Use for any course, onboarding flow or training library that needs to exist in more than one language, or gets updated more than once.

Record once and regenerate forever: fix a mistake without re-recording, update a lesson without reopening a studio, localise the entire course for a new market by swapping the script's language. What used to gate a course's build time on studio availability now runs on the order of 20x faster.

Podcast clipsaudio → video

Use to give audio-only content a face — turning a written or spoken clip into a face-to-camera podcast snippet for social.

The value here is presence: a clip that was just audio now has someone visibly saying it, which is the format most platforms reward.

Brand intros / outrosconsistency

Use for the opening and closing seconds that should look identical across every video a brand puts out.

A consistent, professional-looking open and close no longer costs a reshoot every time the branding changes — update the clone's line once and every future video inherits it.

A/B testing ads without filming againtesting

Use to test tone, pace and confidence, or long-form versus short-form explanations of the same offer — without ever re-booking a shoot.

Because the variable under test is the script rather than the performance, every version starts from the same face and voice — the test measures the message, not a difference in delivery you didn't intend.

06

Advanced time savers

Lesson 3

Past the six use cases sits a smaller set of habits that compound — less about what the clone can be used for, and more about how much of the old production overhead it quietly removes.

01
No more camera days

No lights to set, no framing to check, no retakes, no bad-hair or low-energy day to work around. The clone is always well lit, always on-brand, always consistent, and ready the instant you have a script. That alone saves hours a week that used to go to setup.

02
Script → video in minutes

Write the line in ChatGPT, generate the video. If the hook isn't landing, rewrite and regenerate only the first two seconds — the rest of the video doesn't need to be touched. Swapping a CTA works the same way: change the line, not the take.

03
Infinite versions from one idea

One concept can fan out into three hook styles, two pacing styles, two emotional tones and two CTAs — a dozen distinct videos built from a single idea, none of them requiring a new recording.

04
Clone as your front-end

Put the clone to work explaining the offer, answering FAQs, breaking down pricing, addressing objections, welcoming new users. The face is doing customer-facing work around the clock without the person behind it being present for any of it.

05
Burnout protection

On a day with nothing left in the tank, the posting schedule doesn't know it. The clone still posts, the ads still run, the brand still speaks — output stops being tied to how the person behind it feels that day.

Cloning is the infrastructure, not the content. Every job on this page depends on the same two assets: a trained voice and an uploaded face. Build those once, well, and the rest of the module — HeyGen avatars, ad variants, course libraries — is just what you point them at.
