Japan's Economy: From Bubble to the Yen Crash

A 2-part breakdown of XiaoLinShuo's Japan series — how a postwar miracle built the largest asset bubble in history, why the burst became a 30-year stagnation instead of a recovery, and how that legacy trapped the Bank of Japan in a losing battle against the yen and foreign funds in 2022.

The one thing to hold onto

Rate cuts didn't build Japan's bubble — credit overexpansion did. Financial liberalization pushed companies out of bank lending into bonds, so banks with lending quotas chased junk borrowers, and the resulting debt financed the asset boom. And the bubble burst wasn't what caused the Lost 30 Years — the zombie companies kept alive by rescued banks were. That same debt legacy is why the BOJ couldn't raise rates to save the yen in 2022.

01

The Rise: A Postwar Miracle

XiaoLinShuo · Video 1 · 20分钟详解日本经济泡沫

The keiretsu system — the structural bedrock

After WWII, 40% of Japan's factories and infrastructure lay in ruins. The US occupation (1945-52) rebuilt the country — land reform, labor reform, education reform, delivered at speed — largely out of fear that a destitute Japan would turn socialist. The Korean War then dumped US demand onto Japan, pulling it out of its demand deficit. From 1954 to 1972, Japan averaged over 10% GDP growth per year; per-capita GDP went from under $3,000 (a fifth of the US) to two-thirds of the US by 1973.

The reason Japan's economy behaved unlike any other is the keiretsu (财团) — the corporate groups that replaced the pre-war zaibatsu (财阀) family monopolies after the US dismantled them. Six keiretsu came to dominate half a century of Japanese economic life:

Structure

Bank at the core

Each group is anchored by a major bank. When a group company needs capital, the bank lends on demand. One keiretsu could span electronics, oil, food, autos — 10,000+ companies.

Structure

Cross-holdings

Group companies hold each other's stock, share executives, and coordinate. Textbook crony capitalism — but in good times it works: efficiency, resource pooling, lifetime employment.

Result

Global dominance

Toyota's production system and fuel-efficient cars made Japanese products dominant. Toyota went from 150,000 cars (1960) to 3.3 million (1980). Sony, Toshiba, Panasonic became household names worldwide.

02

The Bubble: Plaza Accord to 39,000

XiaoLinShuo · Video 1

The US countermove

Japan's success became America's problem. After the US left the gold standard and suffered two oil shocks, Fed Chair Paul Volcker's aggressive hikes (fed funds to 20% in 1981) crushed inflation but doubled the dollar and blew out the trade deficit — a third of it from Japan. Trade wars (car import caps, 100% electronics tariffs) didn't fix it, so the US went for the currency.

On September 22, 1985, the US, Japan, France, Britain, and Germany signed the Plaza Accord: coordinated dollar selling. Japan and Germany had to let their currencies appreciate — they agreed because the US was their largest customer. The yen doubled against the dollar in a year: from 250 to 130 per dollar. Japanese exports got twice as expensive overnight. Japan fell into recession in late 1985.

The standard response that built a monster

Japan's textbook response: cut rates. The BOJ cut the benchmark from 5% to 2.5% in 1986-87. By standard logic this was a success — growth recovered, yen appreciation eased, no inflation. The problem wasn't the cut; it was staying at 2.5% too long while the financial system mutated underneath.

03

The Real Mechanism: Credit Overexpansion

XiaoLinShuo · Video 1

Japan had cut rates several times before (1970s, early 80s) with no monster bubble. Why was 1986 different? Because the financial system had changed — this is the subtle version most explanations skip:

1
Financial liberalization
US pressure opened Japan's markets

Japan opened its financial markets, foreign exchange, and — critically — the corporate bond market.

2
Companies fled banks
bonds were cheaper than loans

With rates low, large listed companies found issuing bonds cheaper and easier than bank loans. From 1985-90 corporate debt exploded, driven by bonds, not banks.

3
Banks lost their best customers
but kept their quotas

Low rates already squeezed margins. Now prime borrowers didn't need them. But the BOJ's window guidance (窗口指导) gave banks lending KPIs — lend X billion or else.

4
Banks lent to whoever was left
junk borrowers, then individuals, then anyone

"Put your house up and I'll give you a low-rate loan — if you default, I'll just sell the house." Since prices were rising, banks assumed they couldn't lose.

The result was runaway credit expansion to borrowers with no repayment capacity, at the peak of an asset boom. Then the accounting twist: a 1988 rule let banks book part of their stock gains as current-year profit. As long as stocks rose, banks looked profitable regardless of reality — which pushed stocks higher, which let them borrow more to buy more. Two-thirds of the market ended up as cross-holdings; the keiretsu's lead banks held half the market.

Why the BOJ didn't raise rates sooner: it saw the risk as early as 1987 but held off — the US said no (Louvre Accord, 1987), Black Monday (1987) scared it into inaction, and (per rumor) the Ministry of Finance and the central bank were at odds, with the BOJ dragging its feet deliberately. When it finally acted in 1989, it overcorrected: 2.5% straight to 6%. The Nikkei fell from 39,000 to 17,000 within two years; real estate collapsed in 1991.

04

Why It Took 30 Years to Recover

XiaoLinShuo · Video 1

A normal asset bubble burst knocks you down and you get back up — the US dot-com bust and China's 2007 bubble were painful but recoverable in a couple of years. Japan's problem was the second kick while it was down, delivered by the same keiretsu banking system:

1
Bad loans piled up
collateral collapsed

Borrowers defaulted and the collateral (real estate) was worth a fraction of the loan.

2
The government saved the banks
they hold life savings

Banks can't be allowed to fail. But as keiretsu leaders, they used the rescue money to prop up their failing group companies.

3
Zombie companies
僵尸公司

Firms that should have been liquidated were kept alive by the big brother's bailout money. They crowded out competition, made it impossible to tell good from bad, and dragged good companies down with them. Bank profitability went negative after 1995.

4
The slow-motion collapse
1997-98

Nissan Life Insurance failed in April 1997 (Japan's first postwar insurer bankruptcy). In November 1997, a bank run started — the Finance Ministry ordered everyone inside, paid out whatever was demanded, and Japan's media stayed silent in eerie unison.

The market fell for a decade (39,000 to 8,000 by 2003). Unemployment peaked in 2000, not at the crash. The suicide rate spiked after the 1998 financial crisis. That's the Lost 30 Years.

The dark theory: the book 日元王子 (The Yen Prince) argues the BOJ deliberately wrecked the real economy — the central bank and the Ministry of Finance were deeply hostile, and the BOJ wanted crisis to force reform, discredit the Ministry, and win its own independence. Is it true? Unknown. But it's a reminder that central bank decisions are made by institutions with their own agendas.

05

The Yen Crash: It's the Rate Differential

XiaoLinShuo · Video 2 · 日元崩盘，日本国债闪崩的背后

In 2022, the yen fell to its weakest level since the 1998 Asian financial crisis. The collapse traces almost perfectly to one chart: the spread between US and Japanese 10-year bond yields. The correlation is startling — nearly a perfect match.

The logic is simple. A bond's yield is roughly the return you get for holding that country's currency. When US yields rise relative to Japanese yields, the return on holding dollars beats holding yen, so money flows to the dollar and the yen falls. In 2022: the Russia-Ukraine war spiked oil, US stimulus + rate hikes pushed the 10-year US yield up, the spread widened, and the yen fell from ~115 to 140+ per dollar. Hedge funds made shorting the yen the "trade of the year" — it's a large, liquid market anyone can touch.

Why a falling yen stopped being good news: Japan historically wanted a weak yen (exports cheaper, imports pricier = inflation). But this was a crash, not a controlled decline. Imported inflation made everything more expensive, so people stopped buying — total demand fell. Cost-push inflation is the bad kind. And wild swings scared off international investors, eroding the currency's global standing.

The carry trade: why yen volatility matters so much

The yen's importance in global finance rests on the carry trade. With Japanese rates negative (or near zero) and US rates at 2%+, you borrow yen cheaply, convert to dollars, buy US Treasuries for the higher yield, then convert back later. Since roughly 2000 this has been the world's favorite passive income machine — funds, pensions, and insurers all do it. Before 2008, yen carry trades exceeded $1 trillion.

The catch: carry trades carry currency risk. When you close the trade you must convert dollars back to yen — if the yen has appreciated, your rate gains evaporate. Hedging is expensive enough to kill the arbitrage, so carry traders need a stable currency. The yen was famously stable — which is why it was so beloved. When it started swinging, carry trade investors pulled out and central banks trimmed yen reserves. This is also why the Swiss franc, another low-rate currency, has outsized volume — and why both are "safe haven" currencies: in a crisis, everyone unwinds carry trades by buying the funding currency back, pushing it up.

06

The Battle Over 0.25%: Central Bank vs. Foreign Funds

XiaoLinShuo · Video 2

If the exchange rate tracks the yield spread, the fix seems obvious: raise Japanese rates. But the BOJ was trapped:

Trap 1

QQE contradiction

Japan's entire post-2013 policy was printing money to buy JGBs, capping the 10-year yield at 0.25%. Raising rates to save the yen contradicts the program that defined the era.

Trap 2

The economy can't take it

Aging population, weak credit demand, suppressed aggregate demand. Hiking would punch the economy it's trying to revive.

Trap 3

The debt bomb

Government debt is ~260% of GDP. At near-zero rates, interest payments already consume ~20% of tax revenue. If all yields rose 1%, the government couldn't pay the interest at all.

Trap 4

Politics

2022 was Governor Kuroda's final year. No one at the central bank wanted to be the one who broke the policy.

Most analysts believed the BOJ would never touch the 0.25% cap. Some foreign funds disagreed — and started shorting JGBs and JGB futures, betting yields would break through. The setup was a textbook asymmetric bet: win small if the BOJ held the line (yields stayed capped, shorts lost little), win huge if it blinked (bonds crash, yields spike, shorts clean up). One dollar down to win a hundred — worth the ticket price even at low odds.

So from March 2022, foreign funds shorted JGBs while the BOJ bought everything, fighting to hold 0.25%. The battle escalated through June, producing repeated intraday flash crashes in JGBs — each time the BOJ stepped in and bought them right back. The BOJ has a decisive advantage: unlike Britain in 1992 (Soros vs. the pound, finite FX reserves), Japan prints its own money. Infinite ammunition. In June alone the BOJ bought over a trillion dollars of bonds; it added ~$100 billion of 10-year JGBs between early May and July, forcing yields back under the cap.

The outcome

The BOJ won the bond battle but lost the currency war: the yen's depreciation showed no sign of stopping, and when Japan finally intervened using FX reserves to prop up the yen, the effect was barely visible. The deeper point: Japan's predicament is the bill for 30 years of fighting deflation with mountains of debt and a bloated central bank balance sheet. After half a century of ups and downs, Japan demonstrated to every central bank on earth one uncomfortable truth — not every problem can be solved by printing money.

07

Non-Obvious Takeaways

Both videos
Rate cuts don't cause bubbles by themselves
institutions do

Japan cut rates repeatedly without disaster. What made 1986-89 different: financial liberalization + bond market access + banks with lending quotas + an accounting rule rewarding rising stock prices. The interaction of institutions, not the interest rate alone, built the bubble.

Credit expansion is the real danger, not speculation
borrowed gambling is different

People borrowing to gamble is categorically worse than people gambling with their own money — it converts asset declines into banking-system losses.

Rescuing banks without forcing restructuring creates zombies
the fatal second kick

The 1997 bank-run suppression and the media's silence kept the system alive but rotting. A bubble burst is recoverable; a credit collapse with zombie companies is not.

The yield spread explains the yen move — almost perfectly, for this window
don't over-generalize

The 2022 correlation was unusually tight. Over longer horizons the fit breaks down — many factors move currencies. The rate-differential story is powerful but not universal.

The carry trade makes the yen systemically important
stability was the foundation

Its stability wasn't a side detail — it was the foundation of a trillion-dollar global funding machine. Volatility in the yen is a global financial event, not a Japanese one.

Asymmetric bets against central banks are rational even when doomed
the lottery ticket logic

The shorts knew the BOJ had unlimited firepower. They didn't need to win often — capped downside, explosive upside, and a small chance the central bank would blink made the bet worth buying.

Currency intervention is the weak tool
rates dominate

Japan's FX intervention barely moved the yen. Rate differentials dominate, and Japan's debt load made the rate tool unusable.

Macro dysfunction coexists with a good life
the human reality check

None of this means Japanese people live in misery. High per-capita income, strong social welfare, top-tier happiness indexes, and the world's longest life expectancy. The pain is in the policy box, not the streets.
