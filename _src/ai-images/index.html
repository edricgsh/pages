The model handles beauty. Logic is your job.

Module 01 rebuilt as a working reference: how the generator actually behaves, how to keep a person the same across a hundred images, and the cinematographer's vocabulary — depth, light, camera — that separates a shot that reads as a photograph from one that reads as a render.

The big idea

Every tell that gives AI away — plastic skin, light coming from nowhere, a subject pasted flat onto a backdrop, a face parked dead centre — is a decision you didn't make, not a limit of the model. AI builds flat and evenly lit by default because the prompt gave it nothing else to build with. Direct the frame before you type, and the same model produces a completely different image.

01

Four dials, not one magic sentence

Lesson 1

Almost every AI workflow starts with an image — characters, product shots, ad visuals, thumbnails, avatars, cinematic scenes, and the still frames that later get animated into video. Image generation is never "type a sentence and hope." Strong results come from four things working together, and when an output disappoints, one of the four is the thing to change.

Dial 1

Prompt

What you want created. Can be one line — realistic fitness influencer taking a mirror selfie — or a full description of person, environment, lighting, camera style, clothing, mood, and image type.

Dial 2

Model

How it gets created. The engine. Some models are stronger on realism, some on editing, some are fast and cheap for testing, some handle references better. Switching model is a valid fix.

Dial 3

Settings

Format and quality — aspect ratio, resolution, number of outputs. The same prompt looks materially different on a different canvas shape.

Dial 4

References

Visual context. Instead of describing something in words, you show it. Character consistency, product consistency, clothing, pose, location, style, colour, framing, vibe.

Aspect ratio is chosen before you generate, not after

RatioShapeGoes to
16:9WideYouTube, thumbnails, website headers, landscape and cinematic scenes, character sheets
9:16VerticalTikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, most social video
4:5PortraitInstagram feed image posts

The beginner mistake. Generating half a project in one aspect ratio then switching halfway through. Pick one per project and hold it, unless you genuinely need multiple formats.

References only work if you say what they're for

The most common failure with references isn't a bad reference — it's an unexplained one. Upload a photo without instruction and the model may copy the pose when you wanted only the face, or copy the background when you wanted only the outfit. The prompt tells the AI what to do; the reference shows it what to follow. You have to say which is which.

Use the uploaded image as a character reference.
Keep the same face, hairstyle, skin tone, body type, and overall
identity — but place her in a luxury hotel lobby and change her
outfit to an elegant black dress.
  • Identity: preserve face, hair, skin tone, body type.
  • Wardrobe: preserve the clothing, change the location.
  • Product: preserve packaging, logo, shape, colour, material.
  • Pose only: state explicitly that this is a pose reference, not an identity reference.

Generate, review, adjust, repeat

You are not supposed to get the perfect image every time. Generate several variations from one prompt rather than judging the whole idea from a single output — then use the strongest result as the next thing you improve from. Test on faster, cheaper settings; move to high resolution once the direction works. The failure-to-fix mapping is nearly always mechanical:

SymptomChange
Face is right, outfit is wrongRewrite only the outfit clause
Image looks fake / plasticAdd realistic camera and lighting language
Composition is wrongAdjust the camera and framing instruction
Product drifts from the originalStrengthen the reference instructions, add label text
Nothing worksSwitch model before rewriting again
Assist and Wise Enhance. Both turn a rough idea into a fuller prompt, which is genuinely useful before you're a fluent prompt writer. The catch: enhanced prompts silently add details you didn't ask for. Read the expanded prompt before generating and confirm it still matches your intent. The @ button pulls in saved characters and products so you stop re-uploading the same references.
02

The realism problem — three tells that give AI away

Lessons 1–2

Most AI people fail for the same three reasons, and none of them are about the face being unattractive. They fail because the image is too good in ways real photographs never are. Influencer content is 90% of the time shot on a phone with no control over the background — an image that looks better than that reads as fake.

T1
Airbrushed plastic skin

Nobody is perfect. A flawless avatar is an obvious tell, and skin is the biggest giveaway — zero pores, zero imperfections, uniform smoothness. Prompt for skin texture, a slightly wonky eyebrow, facial asymmetry, anything that reads as an actual human rather than a rendered one.

T2
Perfect studio lighting where it can't exist

A subject standing outdoors with what looks like a ring light on her face. Perfect lighting at all times is physically impossible. A huge part of what makes a photo feel real is the light matching the situation — check that before anything else.

T3
Impossible camera and background

Studio-grade framing and an unnaturally tidy background. Great for a professional shoot, wrong for creator content. Adding shot on an iPhone 15, candid scene, natural framing changes the output dramatically — the background becomes ordinary, like the real world.

The realism lock-in block

Append this to any prompt that needs to pass as a real photograph. It works as a suffix — describe your scene first, then lock the realism.

{YOUR SCENE HERE} — Authentic everyday photorealism, candid and
unposed, natural ambient light appropriate to the setting, realistic
skin texture and material texture, muted true-to-life colours, low
contrast, subtle softness, slight phone-camera or real-camera
imperfection, believable exposure, lived-in details, natural body
language, ordinary real-life styling, imperfect composition, no
glamour retouching, no beauty filter, no cinematic colour grading,
no hyper-polished AI look.

Realism keyword bank

shot on an iPhone 15 candid, unposed realistic skin texture natural ambient light no studio lighting imperfect composition muted true-to-life colours low contrast believable exposure lived-in details natural body language no beauty filter no glamour retouching slightly off-camera expression phone camera feel natural social media photo not overly posed photorealism
One free word. On GPT Image 2.0, end the prompt with photorealism. OpenAI's own engineers flagged that the token carries real weight on realism — there is no cost to including it.
Don't settle. The test is blunt: could this person exist on Instagram, TikTok, or in a UGC ad? If the answer is no, rethink the prompt rather than accepting the best of a plastic batch. A bad base image poisons everything downstream.
03

Building an avatar that stays the same person

Lessons 2–3

The goal is not one good picture. It's a digital person who can show up again and again in different settings, outfits, poses, and content styles. Don't try to create the perfect avatar in one generation — build it in stages, because every strong reference point you create makes the next generation more consistent. At time of recording the models to reach for are ChatGPT Image 2.0 and Nanobanana Pro; the workflow survives whatever replaces them.

01
Rough character idea

Identity, vibe, niche, content world — nothing more. A 23-year-old fitness influencer; a luxury lifestyle creator who shoots in hotels and restaurants; a travel vlogger; a skincare UGC avatar. Keep it simple enough to generate and test fast.

02
Let AI chat write the prompt

Hand Claude or ChatGPT the rough concept and ask for a detailed image prompt. Include niche, age, vibe, content style, realism level, camera style, and platform. Ask for ten variations if you want range — reviewing options is cheaper than writing the perfect prompt.

03
Generate the base face

No reference needed yet — you're still discovering the identity. Generate a batch, don't judge from one output. You're looking for a face that is believable and reusable, not the prettiest one.

04
Run the realism check

Skin texture present? Eyes normal? Human asymmetry? Lighting that matches the situation? If it looks plastic, go back to the prompt — this is the last cheap moment to fix it.

05
Pick the reference image

A clean, realistic portrait, natural lighting, clear view of the face. Not blurry, not heavily filtered, not over-edited, not overly professional. A bad reference leads to a bad result nearly every time, and this one image does most of the work later.

06
Build a character sheet

A turnaround sheet locks every angle so nothing drifts. Generate it in GPT Image 2.0 at 16:9, then upload it as the reference for every future generation. Front, 45° left, 90° left profile, 90° right profile, 45° right, back — full body and matching portrait per column.

07
Prompt new scenes against the sheet

Same person, new location, outfit, camera style, lighting, expression. The identity is anchored by the reference; the prompt only has to describe the scene.

08
Build a content library

Don't stop at two images. Lifestyle posts, product shots, fitness, travel, UGC stills, and later video start frames — stored together so the avatar reads as a real creator profile rather than a one-off.

The character turnaround sheet prompt

Fed alongside your chosen avatar image. The long tail of "do not" clauses is load-bearing — without them the model mirrors portraits incorrectly and drifts the outfit between columns.

hairstyle, hair colour, skin tone, outfit, materials, accessories,

Character description: [INSERT]

Background: neutral plain studio, clean lighting, no environment,
no props unless part of the character design.

Canvas layout: one clean sheet, six vertical columns with thin
borders. Each column contains two stacked images of the same
character — top: full-body view; bottom: matching close-up portrait.
The portrait inherits the full-body rotation exactly: same rotation,
same facing direction, no independent camera angle or pose.

  1  FRONT, 0°     — both eyes visible, chest/hips/knees/feet forward

  4  90° RIGHT     — strict side profile, one eye visible

Influencer Studio — the same workflow, pre-wired

Influencer Studio turns one persona into a content system rather than a pile of unrelated generations. Each influencer gets a dedicated workspace, so the prompt bar already holds the identity — you never re-upload the reference.

Feed

Generation with identity baked in

Prompt the scene only — at the beach holding a cocktail — and the persona holds. Longer prompts still win. Drag past outputs back in as references, or switch to the video tab.

Presets

Batch content categories

Daily aesthetic, luxury, fitness, night out, product placement. Select several at once and generate a whole content pack — eight images without writing a prompt or having an idea.

Captions

Finish the post, not the image

Caption copy plus hashtags, length-tunable. Short and minimal for luxury, motivational for fitness. Add context about what happened, then edit so it still sounds human.

Custom build

When the persona must match a brand

Name, gender, age range, aesthetic, hair and eye colour, plus free text for tattoos, haircut and vibe. Optional references. You get four options — pick the one that survives many outfits and products, not the prettiest.

The scene prompt formula

Action + location + outfit + camera style + look + lighting + mood. Short prompts work; structured prompts work better.

Reference hygiene on custom avatars. Bad references make the avatar worse, not neutral. No blurry faces, no heavy filters, no set of images that look like different people. Every reference you upload must support the same visual direction. And pick the persona for the niche — a muscular gym bro is the wrong base identity for a makeup brand.
04

Product visuals, labels, and the held-product shot

Lessons 4–5

Once the process is understood you can produce brand visuals for almost any product with no studio, photographer, props, location or crew. The whole discipline reduces to one habit: treat the image model as a production assistant, not a magic button. Give it the product, the person, the inspiration, the label text, the scale context, and the job the image has to do.

Pinterest is the direction step, not a shortcut

Pinterest is a visual search engine — you search styles, poses, outfits, camera angles, aesthetics. When you're stuck, never start from a blank prompt. Search the world your content lives in, collect, study, then combine into something original.

luxury lifestyle photoshoot café influencer aesthetic mirror selfie outfit coffee shop photoshoot travel girl aesthetic cinematic product advert skincare campaign fitness brand campaign fashion editorial product image
  • Break each reference into parts. One image gives you the outfit, another the pose, another the framing, background, colour palette, or camera angle. Copying one image whole is the failure mode.
  • Build a bank. Pin to boards or download into a Canva moodboard, grouped by project, brand, character, or content style. Direction comes from the board, not from memory.
  • Never copy a real person's face. When you recreate a campaign shot, change the subject, background, colours and product details — and improve on it.
Prompt translation structure
Subject + action + outfit + location + camera angle + lighting
+ mood + realism details

Example: candid iPhone-style photo of a luxury lifestyle influencer
sitting at a café table, cream outfit, gold jewellery, soft morning
light, relaxed pose, natural background, realistic skin texture.

Swapping products into a scene

The reliable route is two-stage: give an AI chat both images and ask it to write the image prompt, then take that prompt plus the references into the generator. Simple one-line edits do work, but longer prompts are markedly more consistent — especially when the product carries text.

Give me a highly detailed prompt to put the Summer Fridays bottle
into the scene instead of the Fleur Peach skin one. Include details
of all of the text and make it as accurate as possible. Also change
the fruit to mangos and change the background colour to match.
Always type the label. If the product has text on it, the exact text belongs in the prompt. Otherwise you are hoping the model renders it correctly — and it usually doesn't. Same for the product description. This single habit is the difference between gibberish packaging and type that renders cleanly.

The avatar product photo

A product alone is useful. A product held by a believable person toward the camera instantly feels native, social, and ad-ready. But "make the man hold the supplement bottle" produces a blurry bottle with gibberish text almost every time. Order of operations fixes it:

  1. Generate the avatar first — you want control over who is holding it before anything else.
  2. Upload avatar and product together into the chat or generator.
  3. Ask the chat for the prompt rather than writing it cold.
  4. Add multiple product references with size comparisons if proportions or scale keep drifting — the model needs something to measure against.
  5. Check label sharpness, scale, hands and angle before animating anything.
Give me a detailed image prompt where the avatar is holding the
product towards the camera, making sure the product label is sharp,
readable and consistent. Include all text that is on the label in the
prompt and ensure the product proportions are accurate.
Common mistakes. Vague prompts that leave the label, texture, scale and composition to guesswork · uploading a product image without describing its text · attempting the held-product shot without a strong avatar reference first · copying inspiration too directly · accepting the first result when a small prompt improvement would make it usable.
05

The editing stack — what to fix where

Lessons 5–7

Before a still becomes a video it's worth an editing pass. Three tools cover almost everything, and the skill is knowing which one is faster for the job in front of you: a prompt-based edit, a chat with a canvas, or a brush.

Nanobanana — prompt-based image editingGoogle · also in PromptWise

Use when the change is conceptual: relight it, change the season, add an object, swap the era, fuse two images, pose a character.

drag in image → plain-English instruction → refine in turns → each turn preserves the last

Multi-turn editing is the storyboarding superpower: take a castle, make it night, add a pirate ship, drop in a logo generated elsewhere — each step lands with no glitching and the logo is preserved exactly where asked. Multi-image fusion merges a character shot with a separate location or vehicle shot and even colour-corrects the subject to match. Pose control: draw a stick figure, upload it with the character, and it poses them for you. Close-ups hold identity remarkably well — tattoos, a small ear piercing, a football shirt completed from nothing.

Wise Chat — chat, canvas, models in one placefastest loop

Use when you'd otherwise be copying prompts out of a chatbot and re-uploading references into a generator over and over.

upload refs + instruction → Generate button → images land in that chat's canvas → reuse as reference or animate

Works like any chatbot, plus a built-in canvas, image referencing, and every image model inside the same workspace. Each chat keeps its own canvas so nothing gets lost. References you upload attach automatically. The @ symbol addresses anything already in the canvas — "change the woman in image six to be the woman from image three" — and an animate button turns a still into video without leaving the thread.

Canva — the precision and consistency passbrush, not prompt

Use when regenerating is slower or riskier than fixing the one thing that's wrong.

BG Remover · Magic Eraser · Magic Expand · Adjust · Magic Layers

Magic Eraser brushes out a single unwanted object with accuracy a prompt can't match. Magic Expand refits a 9:16 image to 4:5 or reveals more of a body by generating the missing edges. Magic Layers separates a finished image into draggable elements — reposition a subject, move badly placed text, resize and recolour type; ideal for adverts, product shots and thumbnails. And the most underrated step: Adjust to colour-match a sequence, because if the stills don't feel like one world, the video made from them will be worse.

The labelled-collage trick. To add many objects to one image accurately, don't upload them loose. Assemble every object into a single Canva board and label each one, then reference the labels by name in the prompt. Loose uploads are wrong roughly 90% of the time or get refused on guidelines; the labelled board lands a very high object count first try.
Two small ones. Images generated through the Gemini app carry a watermark in the bottom-right corner — remove it with Canva Magic Edit. And don't over-edit in Canva; pushing adjustments too far makes images look worse, not more consistent.

06

Depth — why AI builds flat and how to stop it

Lesson 8

Two shots, almost the same prompt: one looks like cinema, the other looks cheap. It isn't the model. By default AI pastes your subject onto a background like a sticker — one flat layer, nothing in front, no real space behind. That's why it reads as a sticker instead of a moment. The fix happens before you type.

Background
The world behind

Street, room, skyline, landscape, sky. Most people describe only this layer — which is exactly why their shots stay flat. A background on its own is not depth, it's a backdrop.

Midground
Where the subject lives

The person, character, or product — the thing you want looked at. Say explicitly that the subject sits in the midground.

Foreground
The layer everyone forgets

Whatever sits closest to the lens: grass, a window frame, smoke, a shoulder, leaves, a railing, a car hood, a piece of wall — usually slightly out of focus. Adding a foreground is the single fastest way to make any AI shot look cinematic. The eye then travels front → subject → back, and that travel is what reads as three-dimensional.

WEAK
man standing beside an orange sports car in a city street,
cinematic lighting

BETTER
orange sports car close to the camera in the foreground, man
standing beside it in the midground, city street and tall buildings
falling away into the background, cinematic lighting

FOREGROUND ADD-ONS
out-of-focus leaves in the foreground close to the lens · blurred
window frame · grass near the camera · smoke drifting across the
lens · shoulder in the foreground · railing cutting through frame

Atmosphere — something in the air

In the real world air isn't empty. The further something is, the more air sits between you and it, so distant things get lighter, softer, and lose contrast. Your brain reads that instantly as space. It's a cheat code because you can add it on purpose, and each kind gives a different feeling.

  • Light haze in the background pushes a city back and makes the scene feel vast.
  • Dust in a shaft of light gives you god rays and makes the air itself visible.
  • Low fog rolling across the ground physically separates foreground, subject and background so depth is impossible to miss.
  • Smoke or exhaust adds movement and mood on top of the depth.

Where the subject goes

The instinct is dead centre and it's usually the most boring placement available — static, flat, a passport photo. Push the subject onto a third and the rest of the frame becomes space they can look into, walk into, or react to. That space gives the frame direction. Dead centre is still right for a hero shot, raw symmetry, or a stare down the lens — the difference is choosing it on purpose rather than landing there by default.

Rule of thirds

Place, don't default

Split the frame into nine boxes. Put the subject on a line, better still on an intersection. Tell the AI which third — otherwise it centres everything.

Leading lines

Point the eye

Roads, curbs, walls, fences, railings, light beams, rows of parked cars, building edges. Aim them at the subject and the eye has no choice but to slide there.

Lead room

Space in the direction of travel

Looking right, leave room on the right; walking left, leave room on the left. Crowd the space in front and leave it all behind, and the shot feels trapped — like they're about to hit the frame edge.

place the subject on the right third of the frame, looking left
across the open space, strong lead room, cinematic composition,
background receding into fog

industrial street at night, wet reflective ground, low fog rolling
across the street, soft haze in the background, cinematic overhead
lights, subject in the midground
Five-point check before any cinematic prompt. Depth — is it built in three layers? Atmosphere — is there anything in the air? Rule of thirds — did I place the subject or did the model? Leading lines — is anything pointing at the subject? Lead room — does the frame breathe in the right direction? When a shot feels flat, it's almost always the foreground and the air.
07

Light logic — "cinematic lighting" means nothing

Lesson 9

You've scrolled past a thousand AI images that looked off and couldn't say why. Usually it's the light. The model can make a beautiful image, but if the light has no logic the shot still feels random. Writing cinematic lighting tells the model nothing — it responds by lighting everything evenly, which is exactly the render look. Light starts with questions: where is it coming from, why is it here, what is it doing to the frame?

1 · Motivated source

Real light always comes from somewhere — a window, the sun, headlights, a lamp, a doorway, fire, neon, a screen. That source has a direction, and it hits one side harder than the other. The source doesn't have to be in the shot: let the window sit off-frame and let the shadows tell the story, rather than forcing a lamp into the middle and breaking your composition.

WEAK    cinematic lighting, realistic portrait, beautiful room

BETTER  soft daylight from a large window on the left, light falling
        across one side of his face and body, the opposite side
        dropping into soft shadow

2 · Hard or soft, and from where

ChoiceComes fromReads as
Hard lightSmall direct source — bare sun, single sharp lampDramatic, tense, raw, harsh, dangerous. Strong shadows with defined edges
Soft lightLarge or diffused source — big window, overcast skyCalm, expensive, intimate, flattering. Gentle falloff, smooth transitions
Side lightOff to one sideThe most cinematic — carves out shape and texture, one side lit, one in shadow
BacklightBehind the subjectSeparation, rim light, silhouettes, depth
Front lightFlat onto the faceFlattens everything — boring for drama, correct for beauty and fashion: clean skin, no harsh shadows, glossy

The shadow does as much work as the light: the lit side shows the surface, the shadow side gives it shape. You need both. Cinematic light doesn't show everything equally — it chooses what matters and hides the rest.

hard side light from the left, deep shadow on the right,
background darker than the subject, motivated practical light
behind the subject

3 · Colour is the emotion

  • Warm — golden, orange. Alive, intimate, nostalgic. Pulls you in.
  • Cool — blue, grey. Distant, lonely, hard, modern. Holds you at arm's length.
  • Green tint — uneasy, artificial, sick, off. Why so many industrial and institutional scenes carry it.
  • Red — danger, tension, alarm, urgency. Drop a character into red and the mood turns even if nothing else changes.

Orange and blue sit opposite each other on the colour wheel, so a warm subject against a cool background is the strongest colour contrast available — the classic look of warm skin against cool shadows, which separates the subject and adds depth on top of mood. And echo one accent colour through the frame: if the subject has orange details, plant that same orange in a reflection, a light, or a background object. Repetition is what makes an image feel composed rather than random.

Motivate strong colour. Red light reads as cinema when it comes from a safelight, alarm, neon sign, brake light, exit sign or emergency glow. Unmotivated, it reads as a cheap filter.

4 · Materials make it physical

Light doesn't just land on a subject, it shows you what the subject is made of. Metal throws a hard sharp highlight; glossy car paint reflects like a mirror; glass catches light and lets it through; polished stone has a deep smooth sheen; fabric drinks light in, soft and matte; woven carpet catches a little shadow on every thread. Describe how light behaves per surface and the image stops looking like a render. This is decisive for product work — a soft reflection on a glossy black surface is what makes a product look expensive rather than flat. On people, don't lose the catch light: that tiny reflection of the source in the eyes. Without it eyes look dead; with it they look alive — and it has to match your real source.

controlled studio product lighting, sharp specular highlights on
metal, soft reflection on glass, dark reflective surface, subtle
haze behind the product

5 · Continuity across a sequence

One beautiful shot is luck; a scene where the light holds together is skill. The light doesn't have to be physically identical in every frame — the logic has to be. Anchor the source once in the establishing shot and the viewer's brain fills in the rest. Four things stay fixed: where the light comes from, which side is lit, warm or cool, and how deep the shadows go.

The lock-the-block trick. Write your lighting as one block at the end of the prompt. Copy that identical block into every shot in the scene and change only the action and the shot size. If one shot breaks, regenerate it with the same block.

Grading mistakes. "Colourful" means nothing — say which colours and where. Don't stack references; pick one mood, one palette. Don't divorce grade from lighting — you can't have warm golden grading under harsh fluorescents. Don't over-describe: teal and orange cinematic color grading beats a paragraph of RGB values.
Three questions that fix any lit shot. Where is the light coming from? What is it doing to the frame? Does it hold in the next shot? The beauty the model can do; the logic is yours. When the light has a reason, the viewer stops noticing the light and simply believes the scene.
08

Camera language — angle, shot size, lens

Lesson 10

The same person, in the same place, wearing the same thing can feel powerful or vulnerable, intimate or distant, in control or lost — and that entire shift can come from the camera alone. The camera isn't recording the scene, it's telling the audience how to read it. Three tools, three questions.

Tool 1 — Angle: where is the viewer standing?

Not just up or down: how high the camera sits, which side of the subject it's on, and whose eyes we're borrowing.

AngleReads as
Eye levelThe honest baseline. Intimate or confrontational depending on the moment
Low angleThe subject towers — powerful, heroic, sometimes threatening. How you make someone own the room
Worm's-eyeCamera on the floor aiming straight up. Extreme on a person, a car, a bike, a building
High angleThe subject shrinks — vulnerable, exposed, cornered
Overhead / top-downThe scene becomes geometry. Rooms become diagrams, people become pieces on a board
Dutch angleHorizon tilted. Instantly wrong, tense, unstable
FrontalDirect, symmetrical, iconic. Eyes in the lens = talking to the viewer
Three-quarterShape and depth. The most natural cinematic look
ProfileDistant, locked in, closed off
Back shotMystery. You follow them into the scene, seeing what they see but not their face
Over-the-shoulderHow you shoot a conversation or confrontation — whoever's shoulder is larger quietly holds the power
POVThe camera becomes the character's eyes. Sell it with what they'd see: hands, a steering wheel, glasses frames, a helmet visor, a scope, a keyhole
No angle has one fixed meaning. A low angle usually says power — but on a scared child looking up at an adult, that same angle says fear. A high angle usually says weakness, but it can also just show order and control. The angle sets the direction; the character, the light and the moment decide the final meaning.

Tool 2 — Shot size: what does the viewer need to read?

Shot size is not about far or close. It decides what information the viewer is supposed to take from the frame.

SizeFramesThe viewer reads
Extreme wideSubject is a dotThe world, its scale, how small the person is inside it. Opens a scene or crushes a character
WideHead to toe, lots of spacePerson and place at once. Put the character and a threat in one wide frame and the viewer sees the danger first — that's suspense before a single cut
Full shotHead to toe, frame ownedBody, posture, silhouette, outfit, stance
Medium-wideAround the kneesAction and movement — face, hands, body and some surroundings
MediumFrom the waistThe natural distance of conversation. The most versatile size — and monotonous if a whole scene sits here
Medium close-upChest or shouldersThe shift from behaviour to reaction. The shot of connection and trust
Close-upFace fills the frameEyes, breath, the smallest change. Not automatically emotional — the same close-up can be cold, threatening, beautiful or empty
Extreme close-upOne detailA single eye, a drop of sweat, a finger on a trigger, a crack in metal. For that moment nothing else exists

A shot's power depends on what came before it. A close-up only really hits after you've held the viewer at a distance. If every shot is tight, the close-up stops being special — a huge wide followed by a hard cut to a single eye is where the tension lives.

Tool 3 — Lens: how does the space feel?

Focal lengthBehaviourUse for
16–24mmSwallows space, exaggerates depth, enlarges the foreground, puts the viewer inside the sceneInteriors, POV, cars, action
40–50mmRoughly human vision. Calm proportions, no optical trickNatural, neutral coverage
85mmNarrower. Separates subject from background while pulling the background visually closerPortraits, subject separation
200mm+Compresses space, stacks distant planes. Feels like watching from far away — lets you put a giant sun right behind a characterCompression, distance, scale tricks
The rule that separates optics from typing numbers. Focal length controls the field of view; camera position controls perspective. A wide lens does not distort a face by itself — the distortion appears when you shove the camera into the face to get the close-up. Describe both the lens and where the camera stands.

Aperture confuses everyone because a small number means a big opening. At f/1.4 the lens is wide open: lots of light, shallow depth of field, blurry background. At f/8 most of the scene stays sharp. But a person standing against a wall stays fairly sharp even at f/1.4 — pull them ten metres off that wall and the background melts. Subject-to-background distance matters as much as the f-number, so put it in the prompt.

The three questions. Where is the camera? — angle, which sets relationship and power. How much do we see? — shot size, which controls information. How does the space feel? — lens, which controls the geometry. Answer them yourself and the frame is yours, not the model's.
