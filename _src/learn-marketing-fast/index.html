The 6 Moves The Reframes Checklist

Six moves from shy kid to CMO thinking.

Joanna Wiebe's answer to “how do I learn marketing in 2026?” — the exact plan she'd follow to rebuild a seven-figure career from zero. The through-line: think like a CMO from day one, which mostly means asking different questions than the people around you — performance or growth, will AI cite this, where does the message need to live, what would make this forgettable.

The big idea

None of the six moves is a channel, a tool, or a tactic. Each one is a level of thinking: growth over performance, AI as pressure tester not typewriter, citation over traffic, specialization over generalism, ecosystem over content, and point of view over polish. The marketers who win in 2026 aren't the ones who execute fastest — they're the ones whose thinking was challenged before they started.

01

The six moves — the story, the shift, the tactic

Full video · 13 min
1
Pick your game
The story: Adobe killed the one-time purchase and went subscription. Backlash, stock dip — then recurring revenue stabilized the business, smaller creators could finally afford it, and the customer base exploded. The shift: performance marketing makes what exists work better (conversion, CAC, ads, copy); growth marketing expands what's possible (new channels, audiences, revenue models). Beginners land in performance roles because that's where the entry jobs are — fine, but performance-only thinking optimizes someone else's vision. Tactic: before any task, ask — “Am I here to make something existing work better, or build something that doesn't exist yet?”
2
Use AI like a strategist
The story: the first wave replaced writers with AI, got generic slop, and came back — not for faster typists, but for marketers who could stress-test a position and predict what happens two steps after going to market. The survivors weren't the ones who used AI most; they used it for business problems, not wordsmithing. The shift: CMOs know AI's greatest use isn't production, it's pressure testing. Most marketing fails because the thinking behind it was never challenged. Tactic: ask AI “where does this fall apart?” not “can you write this?” — and as a beginner, upload your work, describe who you're selling to, and ask it to find every place a real customer would tune out. Wiebe calls that one habit stronger than anything else on the list.
3
Learn the new SEO
The story: the 2018 Medic update buried businesses overnight — the lesson “don't build on rented land” — and everyone kept building on rented land anyway, just different land: Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn. Now buyers ask ChatGPT questions Google was never good at answering, and AI answers directly without sending anyone to your site. The shift: a new discipline, answer engine optimization. The currency moved from traffic to citation — getting ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity to cite you as the source of truth. Wikipedia, Reddit, and YouTube are where AI tools pull from: structured, referenceable content. Tactic: stop asking “will Google rank this?” — ask “will AI cite this?” Is it referenceable, not just shareable or viral or engaging? If no AI would pull it for your ICP, don't create it anyway.
4
Specialize or be replaced
The story: HubSpot and Moz didn't win at marketing — they invented the inbound category and became synonymous with it. The marketers you actually know are all specialists: April Dunford on positioning for enterprise B2B, Dave Gerhardt on founder-led marketing. The shift: the adaptable-generalist advice is now the replaceable path. Spread across every channel and you're slightly okay at all of it — “if everyone knows the same things, you're basically a walking AI tool. You can be prompted and replaced.” Tactic: pick one thing, go deep enough that people cite your frameworks — as narrow as scripting videos or owning LinkedIn outreach. You don't need to be an expert to start: a weekly newsletter sharing what you're learning, figuring it out in public, makes you the recognized voice in your corner in three years. Broader = forgettable. Specific = promotable. Narrow = loud signal.
5
Own the ecosystem
The story: Tim Hortons isn't the best coffee or the fanciest brand — it sponsors hockey leagues, its logo is on jerseys and inside rinks, so a cold arena at 5am feels wrong without a cup in your hand. Nike does the same at scale: athlete partnerships, league sponsorships, designer collabs, community platforms. The shift: every one of those is a distribution decision, not a content decision. They're not asking how to make a better post; they're asking how to become part of the identity of the person who buys. Junior marketers are handed a channel and told to make it perform; the head of marketing thinks one level above — where does the message need to live for the right person to feel it at the right moment. Tactic: stop asking “how do I improve this post?” — ask “how do we embed ourselves into our customers' lives?” Be the person in the room asking it.
6
Don't be boring
The story: Doodle's emails were technically correct, professionally written, and completely invisible. Wiebe's team wrote emails unusual enough that people didn't know what to make of them — users wrote in to say they loved them, others to say they hated them, support was furious about the reply volume, and conversion rates skyrocketed. The shift: they worked because they took a position. A point of view means saying something that can be disagreed with — which means saying something real. In a world where most marketing sounds committee-written to offend no one, real is remarkable. Tactic: the most common mistake isn't saying the wrong thing, it's saying nothing worth remembering. If a line came easily, it sounded like everything else — cut it. Use the words real customers use when frustrated. And reframe the creative brief: ask “what would make this completely forgettable?” then do the opposite — generic language, no point of view, avoided tension.
02

The four questions that change your level

Scattered through the video

Strip the six moves down and they're all the same operation: replacing the question everyone else is asking with a bigger one. These are the four swaps worth memorizing — each one moves you from executor thinking to CMO thinking.

Performance or growth?before every task

“Am I here to make something existing work better, or am I here to build something that doesn't exist yet?” Know which one you're doing and you know exactly how to think about it. Most marketers spend their whole career in the first question.

Will AI cite this?before every piece of content

Replaces “will Google rank this?” The currency moved from traffic to citation. Create content the answer engines want to reference — structured, referenceable, source-of-truth material — not content that just collects clicks.

What would make this forgettable?before writing anything

Replaces “how do I make this more creative?” The answer is always the same: generic language, no point of view, all tension avoided. Once you can see boring, doing the opposite gets easy.

Where does this need to live?instead of “how do I improve this post?”

Replaces the channel-level question with the ecosystem question: how do we embed ourselves into our customers' lives, so we're inside the conversation before it starts? That's a distribution decision — the message is table stakes.

03

The operating checklist

Applied to day-to-day work
1
Before every task
Name the game: performance or growth? If you're optimizing, know whose vision you're optimizing.
2
Weekly habit
Upload your copy to AI, describe the buyer, ask it to find every place a real customer tunes out. Fix what it finds.
3
Every piece of content
Is it referenceable? Would an AI answer engine cite it when your ICP asks a question? If not, kill it.
4
Career moat
One thing you want to be known for (not just can do) + a public record of how you think about it, published weekly. Three years, and it compounds in a way job titles never will.
5
Distribution
Before improving any asset, ask where the message needs to live to meet the customer — then decide if the asset even matters.
6
Before shipping
What would make this completely forgettable? If the line came easily or a committee would approve it, cut it. Say something that could be disagreed with.
