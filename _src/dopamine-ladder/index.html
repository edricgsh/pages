Six rungs between a scroll and a superfan.

Kallaway's attention framework claims a single mechanism behind everything that holds a viewer: every video a person watches releases dopamine in six escalating stages, and if you can march a viewer through all six consistently, you condition them the way Pavlov conditioned his dogs — their mouth waters (their thumb taps) at the sight of your name alone. This is the full framework: the psychology underneath each rung, the tactics that trigger it, the reason education content beats entertainment at the top of the ladder, and a real short walked through level by level.

The big idea

The ladder splits into two halves. Levels 1–4 are the message — stimulation, captivation, anticipation, validation — and they are purely a property of the individual video: stun the eye, pop a question, string the viewer along, then close the loop. Levels 5–6 are the messenger — affection and revelation — and they are a property of the person behind the video. Almost anyone can learn the first four. The last two are where attention stops being a craft and becomes a brand, because level six is the Pavlovian endgame: dopamine on sight, before a single frame plays.

Each rung releases more dopamine than the last. Levels 1–4 are engineered inside the video itself (the message); levels 5–6 live in the creator's identity (the messenger) and compound across videos. The dashed line is the dopamine curve: weakest at stimulation, peak at revelation.

01

The prequel — six stages, one conditioned response

00:02 – 01:55

Before the ladder itself, the claim that makes it worth building: a viewer's journey through a piece of content passes through six stages, each releasing more dopamine than the last. Ascend them all and they watch the whole video and want more. Ascend them consistently across videos and you create what Kallaway calls the ultimate goal of content — a Pavlovian effect.

The analogy is deliberate and literal. Pavlov rang a bell, gave a dog food, and eventually the bell alone made the dog salivate. Content works the same way: your name and face are the bell, the dopamine hit is the food, and the saliva is the tap. The endgame state —

“The ultimate sign of attention mastery is when you can make dopamine release in the brain of a viewer just by seeing your name or face without actually watching any of your content.”

That is the definition of a super fan, and Kallaway calls it “king status in attention land.” It's the mechanism behind every creator whose uploads you open on reflex, and it's also why he claims the skill is transferable: learn to trigger it on command and you can “pretty much build any business as big as you want.” The rest of the video is the how.

02

Level 1 — Stimulation: the visual stun gun

01:56 – 04:16

Stimulation is what happens in the first one to two seconds, and it's identical for short-form and long-form. Think of it as a visual stun gun: the deer in the woods that notices something out of the corner of its eye and can't resist looking up and locking in. That split-second lock-in is the whole job of rung one — you're not earning their interest yet, you're earning the space to get to level two.

The dopamine here is pre-cognitive. It comes from colors, motion, and brightness picked up by the eye before the brain comprehends anything. Kallaway demonstrates with eight blurred images: eyes go first to the ones with more motion, color, and brightness, and you can't override it — it's subconscious. His grayscale test makes the same point: watch your feed in black and white and it stops being interesting.

Under the hood, this is bottom-up processing: a rapid subconscious stage that runs in about 200 milliseconds, catching vivid colors in motion without comprehending what it's seeing. The tactical translation:

Enough color, motion, and contrast to visually stun in the first 1–2s Your own palette, motion, and aesthetic — don't copy the feed Desensitization kills copied patterns: everyone's version stops working

The catch is the desensitization loop. If everybody copies the same motion-and-color pattern, the whole feed carries it, viewers get desensitized, and it stops working. That's why the advice is to build a unique visual identity — a signature palette, motion language, and aesthetic — because that's what cuts through a saturated feed. Stimulation is the weakest and shortest dopamine release of the six, but it's the necessary precursor: no stun, no ladder.

03

Level 2 — Captivation: pop a question in their head

04:17 – 07:34

Captivation is where comprehension starts mattering — what you say and how you say it. This is the curiosity stage, and the mechanism is the curiosity loop: the viewer asks an open question in their own head, and the act of opening that loop releases a massive hit of dopamine. The human brain is a problem-solving machine; it evolved to take open, complex questions and solve them, and it rewards itself for doing so.

Two variables scale the reward:

1
Size & non-obviousness
The bigger and more non-obvious the question, the bigger the curiosity, the higher the stakes, the more dopamine.
2
Relevance
The more relevant to the viewer, the bigger the curiosity. His example: “when are the aliens visiting Earth?” is huge but irrelevant to most people — they churn anyway.

So while structuring the hook, run two checks: what is the most curiosity-inducing question I could ask about this topic? and how well does that question map to something my ideal viewer cares about? There are two ways to implant the question — ask it rhetorically, or imply it with details so the viewer assembles it themselves — but either way it has to pop fast, in the opening seconds, or the viewer bounces.

His counter-example is a makeup tutorial: the visuals stun him (level one works), then “here's how you do your makeup for the holidays” pops the question “how does she do it?” — and he churns because he doesn't care about makeup. If your hooks aren't hooking, the question that popped was either irrelevant or not interesting enough.

Two pro tips for amplifying curiosity: use contrast (compare a known thing to an unknown thing), and frame a shocking or weird premise the viewer has never seen. Unknown things pop curiosity better than anything else, because curious people want to identify the thing they've never seen.

04

Level 3 — Anticipation: the edging of storytelling

07:35 – 10:26

Anticipation is what happens when the viewer starts guessing the answer to the question you popped. It's a mystery novel playing out in their head in real time — a subconscious guessing game. And the key property of the curve is that the highest dopamine of the whole loop arrives just before the answer, not at it.

So the craft is in the approach. Give the viewer details that let them anticipate — that ratchets curiosity up — and then, just before you deliver, yank the answer away and head-fake them in another direction. Misdirection, non-obvious twists, and head fakes reset the loop: they guess, get distracted, pop a new question, and the seesaw of question → anticipation → answer starts again. That's why it's called a loop. (The alternative is softer: give the answer, then immediately pop a second question.) Kallaway's phrasing, unflinching: this is “the edging of the storytelling world.”

The canonical example is the truck hook — the clip where a car slowly approaches someone who looks about to get hit. The first frame pops the question (“is he going to get hit?”), and every second of the car's approach is anticipation releasing dopamine in real time. It works because it plays on an anticipatory instinct.

One big constraint: the viewer can only anticipate if they're clear on the facts. Rogue details that don't connect to the question, or facts they can't parse, produce confusion, not curiosity — and confused viewers bounce. Three structuring questions before you write:

1
What should they expect?
Based on the question from level two, what answer should the viewer be anticipating?
2
What gets them closer?
Which additional facts or context move them closer to that answer?
3
Pay off or head-fake?
Give the answer now, or tease them away from it to reset the loop?
05

Level 4 — Validation: close the loop, but not predictably

10:27 – 11:58

Validation is the payoff — you finally answer the question you popped in level two. The goal is to reward with something non-obvious, something the viewer couldn't have fully predicted. For entertainment content, validation is the resolution of the story: no cliffhangers, loose ends wrapped, the truck victim safe. The closed loop signals “story complete,” and completion releases its own dopamine.

For education content, validation is the concrete tip, story, or recommendation the viewer can actually use to solve a problem. This is why the loop structure is non-negotiable: a viewer who leaves the flow without the loop closed is unsatisfied and never gets the final release — they remember the video as a waste.

Walk the cycle in order: curiosity, question, anticipation (what could the answer be?), validation (here's the answer). With levels one through four you can structure any script and any visual story. These four are the base game — they're all about the video itself, the message. The last two rungs are where the ladder stops being about the video and starts being about you.

06

Level 5 — Affection: the viewer starts liking you

11:59 – 13:57

Affection is where the viewer begins to like the creator, not just the content. As soon as they realize they like and trust the person delivering the message, dopamine releases again — and it buys you a longer leash. A stranger's video gets churned in seconds; a creator you already like gets minutes. And because the viewer recognizes it's you, the likability carries over from video to video.

This is why faceless content hits a ceiling: it can do the first four levels easily, but with no personality to attribute taste, trust, and like to, it's hard to ascend to the Pavlovian peak.

Four levers for likability, in ascending order of strength:

1
Attractiveness
Massive, subconscious, and unfair — “I hate to say it as someone with a face for radio,” but attractive people get liked more. If you have it, lean into it: show yourself more.
2
Vibe
The way you look, dress, sit, and carry yourself. Silent signals that feed the like/dislike decision.
3
Smiling
Joy transfers through the lens. If you look happy and passionate, viewers feel that warmth themselves.
4
Solving their problem
The strongest and easiest. Actually help someone with something they're struggling with and trust and likability max out, agnostic of attractiveness or smile.

The closing note is anti-hack: don't force it. Be authentically passionate and live it out; the more value you deliver, the more likable and trustworthy you seem. Authenticity is itself the lever.

07

Level 6 — Revelation: you become the Pavlovian bell

13:58 – 17:16

Revelation is the moment the viewer realizes you will be a continued source of value over time. When that lands, you've trained them: you equal value, so seeing you releases max dopamine — the Pavlovian response, fully formed. Kallaway's analogy is dating: when you've spent enough time with someone who always makes you feel good, hearing their name alone triggers a hit of positive feeling. That's the state he's after — the name is the bell.

The asymmetry that matters: both entertainment and education content can climb from level one to five, but education climbs five to six far more easily. Entertainment only reaches revelation if the viewer decides you're a consistent source of maximum entertainment — every single piece above their personal line. That's the Mr. Beast bar, and anyone below it is competing with Netflix, the NFL, and Mr. Beast himself. Education has a cheaper route: solve specific problems people actually have, and they tie you to a consistent source of value in that area.

“If you solve specific problems for people that they have in their life, they will tie you with a consistent source of value. And when they have this revelation, it releases max dopamine whenever they see you — because you've trained that you equal value.”

How fast you get there scales with pain. A viewer deeply struggling with your topic might ascend one to six in two pieces of content. A viewer for whom the topic is nice-to-have needs many more. The engine in both cases is the same: deliver non-obvious, differentiated value, over and over and over.

08

Worked example — Citizen's shoelace short, all six levels

17:18 – 20:56

To make the ladder concrete, Kallaway walks through a real short from Citizen, a musician who uses viral stitch clips to market his music. The clip: a couple hugs, the girl gets in a car, her shoelace is stuck in the door — and the guy gets dragged down the street, singing. Watch it through the rungs:

1
Stimulation
Slight zoom in, two people hugging, a camera perspective from inside a car — something you don't normally see on the feed. Enough to narrow focus for a second.
2
Captivation
Questions fire in sequence: who are these people? why are they hugging? do I care? (almost a churn) — then the on-screen text “Bro, what did I just witness?” pops a new question: what are we about to see? Then the shoelace: is he aware? is she going to drive away? Three or four rapid open loops.
3
Anticipation
He sees he's stuck — is he going to get out or get dragged? The car pulls away, he goes down: is he being dragged? Are they actually going to film this? The guessing game runs in real time.
4
Validation
He falls backwards as expected, then a hard match cut to him being dragged — singing, audio in. Not what you expected. The loop closes (he's okay) and the head-fake releases its own dopamine; new loops open (why is he doing this? does this music-video strategy work?).
5
Affection
First video of his, so there's not enough to truly like or trust him — entertainment content makes this rung slow. But the clip is funny and shocking, and that hat-tip is enough to make you watch the next one to see what stunt he pulls.
6
Revelation
Only after several videos do you realize the category: viral marketer meets music. If you like marketing or his music, every new video is a promised drip of that unique entertainment — the association forms. Slow for entertainment, but the point stands: he engineered each piece to trigger the loops that kept you watching.

The closing test for the whole framework: take any educational content that has ever worked on you, run it through these six levels, and the reason you liked it will be visible level by level. The job is just to reverse-engineer that structure into your own content.

09

The ladder as a checklist

20:56 – 22:23

The framework is meant to be used operationally, as a checkpoint you run every video against. Before publishing, walk the rungs and audit your answers:

1
Stimulation
Is there enough color, motion, and contrast in the first 1–2 seconds — with a look that's yours, not the feed's?
2
Captivation
What question pops in the viewer's head? Is it big enough and relevant enough to their actual life?
3
Anticipation
Am I feeding details that let them guess, and am I head-faking before the answer to reset the loop?
4
Validation
Did I close the loop with something non-obvious — a resolution or a tip they can actually use?
5
Affection
Did I give them a reason to like and trust the person behind the video?
6
Revelation
Is this one more deposit in the “you equal value” account — is the viewer closer to salivating at your name?

“Are you driving the right curiosity? Are you driving the right dopamine releases?” — that's the whole audit. Content that keeps getting watched is not mysterious; it's a ladder that was climbed on purpose.
