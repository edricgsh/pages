Tariffs, Trade & The New World Order

A 3-part breakdown of XiaoLinShuo's economics trilogy — from how a tariff actually works, to why the US structurally must run a trade deficit, to what Trump's team is actually trying to do and why it keeps contradicting itself.

The one thing to hold onto

The US dollar's role as the world's reserve currency forces the US to run a trade deficit. That deficit funds the global economy. Trump's strategy tries to unwind this structural feature — using tariffs as leverage — but the playbook is full of internal contradictions that make the core goal harder to achieve the harder it's pushed.

How Tariffs Work

XiaoLinShuo · Video 1

The basic mechanism

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. When a country imposes a 20% tariff on milk powder, the original $100 price becomes $120 for the domestic consumer — $20 goes to government revenue, and $100 still goes to the foreign producer.

The milk tea example — a 20% tariff splits between government revenue and consumer cost.

Who wins and who loses

Winner

Government

Collects tariff revenue on every unit imported. A larger tax base for public spending.

Winner

Domestic Producers

Shielded from cheaper foreign competition — they can charge higher prices and hire more workers.

Loser

Consumers

Pay more for the same goods. US washing machine buyers paid 34% more after 2018 tariffs. The burden is visible and immediate.

Why tariffs still exist despite the economic loss

Classical economics says tariffs create deadweight loss — a net welfare reduction where the gains to government and producers are smaller than the loss to consumers. So why do they persist?

Infant industry protection
historical case

Alexander Hamilton championed US tariffs above 40% in the 1800s to shield nascent American industry. Steel output grew 500-fold. Protection at the right moment can lock in a comparative advantage.

Negotiation leverage
strategic use

Tariffs serve as credible threats. You can't negotiate a reduction in someone else's tariffs unless you have tariffs of your own to offer in return. The threat is the tool.

Political appeal
domestic dynamics

Winners (domestic producers, unions, local governments) are concentrated and vocal. Losers (consumers) are diffuse and largely unaware of the source of the price increase. The politics favour protection even when the economics don't.

Retaliation risk
the chicken-truck war

In 1963, the US imposed 25% tariffs on light trucks after Europe taxed US chicken exports. Europe retaliated. The tit-for-tat is now permanent — American truck buyers still pay that tariff today. Escalation is easy; de-escalation is slow.

02

The Tariff Game

Game Theory · Prisoner's Dilemma

Trade between two countries is a prisoner's dilemma. Each can choose to trade openly or to protect their market with tariffs. The payoff matrix shows why rational actors end up in mutual loss.

Country B — Open Country B — Protect
Country A — Open A +3, B +3 — mutual gain (best collective outcome) A −5, B +8 — A exploited, B gains
Country A — Protect A +8, B −5 — A gains, B exploited A −3, B −3 — Nash equilibrium (mutual loss)

The Nash equilibrium is mutual protection — each country's rational best response to any choice by the other is to protect. Without coordination, both end up worse off than if they had both stayed open.

Escaping the trap

1
Tit-for-tat

In a repeated game, cooperate on the first move, then mirror your opponent exactly. It punishes defection immediately and rewards cooperation — and in real-world simulations it outperforms every other strategy. This is the logic behind trade retaliation: you must respond to tariffs with tariffs, or the other side learns they can exploit you freely.

2
Institutions

The WTO exists to solve the prisoner's dilemma at scale. With 166 member countries covering 98% of global trade, it creates rules, dispute mechanisms, and reputational stakes that make defection costly. It substitutes multilateral trust for bilateral suspicion. When the US bypasses the WTO, it dismantles the very mechanism that makes open trade stable.

03

300 Years of Global Trade

Historical timeline

The story of trade policy is a story of pendulums — protection during industrialisation, openness once an advantage is locked in, retrenchment when the gains seem to stop flowing your way.

Mercantilism
1700s–1800s

Britain maintained average tariffs around 50%. The game was to maximise exports, minimise imports, and accumulate gold. Every nation was in a zero-sum race. The theory eventually collapsed under Ricardo's insight about comparative advantage.

Comparative Advantage
Ricardo, 1817

David Ricardo showed that both countries gain from trade even if one is better at producing everything — as long as each specialises in what it does relatively best. Trade is not zero-sum. This idea underpinned the push for free trade for the next two centuries.

US and Germany Industrialise
1800s

Both countries used high tariffs (above 40%) to build domestic industries from scratch — exactly the "infant industry" argument Hamilton made. Steel, textiles, and manufacturing were protected until they were competitive. Free trade came after the advantage was secured, not before.

Smoot-Hawley
1930

The US raised tariffs across the board during the Depression. Trading partners retaliated. Global trade collapsed by 66%. The episode is the canonical cautionary tale — protectionism in a downturn accelerates the contraction instead of cushioning it.

GATT and Free Trade
1947 onward

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade brought average tariffs from ~22% down to ~5% over decades. Global exports grew from 5% to 25% of GDP. This was the era that most economists treat as "normal" — and which is now being actively unwound.

US–China Trade War
2018 – present

The Trump administration imposed tariffs on $360B of Chinese goods. China retaliated. Supply chains began moving to Vietnam, Mexico, and India. The word "decoupling" entered mainstream vocabulary. Biden kept the tariffs and added semiconductor export controls. The pendulum is swinging back.

04

The US Trade Deficit & the Triffin Dilemma

XiaoLinShuo · Video 2

The US has run a current account deficit continuously since the 1970s. By 2024 it exceeded $1 trillion per year. This is not a policy failure. It is a structural consequence of the dollar's role in the global financial system.

The circular flow

When foreign countries sell goods to the US, they earn dollars. Their central banks then reinvest those dollars in US Treasury bonds — the safest dollar-denominated asset. That capital inflow strengthens the dollar. A stronger dollar makes US exports more expensive and imports cheaper, which widens the trade deficit further. This loop has been running for 50+ years.

Foreign exports → USD → Central banks buy Treasuries → Stronger USD → Wider deficit ↺

The self-reinforcing loop — dollar strength and the trade deficit feed each other indefinitely.

The Triffin Dilemma

Robert Triffin predicted in the 1960s that the country issuing the world's reserve currency will inevitably run a trade deficit. The world needs dollars to conduct international trade. The US must supply those dollars by buying more than it sells. This is not a bug in US policy — it is a structural feature of being the reserve currency issuer. You cannot fix the deficit without giving up the dollar's reserve status. You cannot keep the reserve status without accepting the deficit.

Healthy vs unhealthy deficits

Not all trade deficits are equal. The distinction is what the inbound capital is financing.

Healthy

Australia

Ran a deficit for decades while foreign investment poured into productive assets — mines, infrastructure, real estate. The capital inflow funded growth. When the debt was called, the economy had expanded enough to service it.

Unhealthy

Greece

Foreign capital financed consumption and public spending, not productive investment. When the capital flows reversed during the 2010 crisis, there was no expanded productive base to draw on. The deficit was a symptom of structural dependency, not growth.

05

Trump's New Trade Strategy

XiaoLinShuo · Video 3

The Trump 2025 trade strategy is based on the Stephen Milan framework — an economic blueprint that explicitly ranks seven policy goals. Economic growth, which has topped every US administration's priority list since Reagan, is no longer number one.

The seven goals, ranked

Government finances
core priority

Reducing the fiscal deficit and servicing debt. Tariff revenue is part of the funding plan. The US runs a $1.8T annual deficit on a $29T GDP — the math is getting harder as interest rates rise.

Manufacturing revival
core priority

Reshoring supply chains seen as strategically critical — semiconductors, steel, pharmaceuticals, defence components. Tariffs price foreign competitors out and make domestic production viable even at higher cost.

Restricting China
core priority

Slowing Chinese access to US technology, capital markets, and supply chains. The 145% China tariff rate announced in April 2025 was partly a de facto trade embargo on specific categories.

Economic growth
compromise goal

Desired, but explicitly sacrificeable if it conflicts with the core priorities. A radical departure from every US administration since the 1980s, which treated GDP growth as the north star of economic policy.

Dollar hegemony
sacrificeable

The framework explicitly accepts dollar weakening as a tool — something no prior administration would have said out loud. A weaker dollar makes US exports cheaper and could help close the trade deficit. The Triffin logic suggests this would require giving up the reserve currency status.

The Milan Formula

The strategic playbook: impose very high tariffs on a broad set of trading partners, then use the threat of those tariffs as leverage to extract concessions — lower their tariffs on US goods, purchase more US exports, accept security arrangements, or pay for US military presence.

The April 2 "Liberation Day" tariff announcement was the opening move. The market reacted: 10-year Treasury yields spiked 60 basis points in a single week — a bond crash that signalled the world was questioning US fiscal credibility, not just trade policy.

Mar-a-Lago Accordproposed framework

Multilateral dollar devaluation with allies

Convene allied nations (G7-style) and agree to jointly weaken the dollar, in exchange for the US maintaining its security umbrella. The precedent is the 1985 Plaza Accord, where the US, Japan, France, Germany, and the UK agreed to depreciate the dollar to reduce the US trade deficit. The 2025 version would require allies to accept dollar weakness and buy US goods in return for continued US military protection.

Unilateral Strategyfallback

Levy a fee on foreign Treasury holdings

If allies won't cooperate, the unilateral version: charge foreign central banks a fee for holding US Treasuries, effectively taxing the reserve-currency privilege back. This would force dollar weakening without multilateral consent — but risks triggering a flight from Treasuries that would spike US borrowing costs.

07

The Contradictions

Why the strategy might fail

The Milan Framework has internal tensions that become more acute the harder the strategy is pushed. Each goal, pursued aggressively, tends to undermine one or more of the other goals.

Tariff Paradox #1
fiscal vs inflation

Tariffs raise consumer prices → inflation rises → the Fed must hike rates → US debt servicing costs explode. This directly contradicts goal #1: fixing government finances. The tariff revenue is far smaller than the increased interest burden.

Tariff Paradox #2
manufacturing vs dollar

Tariffs attract capital inflows (investors want to be inside the tariff wall) → the dollar strengthens → US exports become expensive → manufacturing reshoring is undermined. The tool meant to revive manufacturing makes manufacturing less competitive.

Dollar Paradox
hegemony vs devaluation

The strategy wants allies to sell their dollar reserves (to weaken the dollar) while simultaneously maintaining the dollar as the world's reserve currency. These are contradictory. A reserve currency must be trusted as a store of value — you cannot weaponise it against your allies and expect them to hold it anyway.

Fed Paradox
rates vs inflation

Trump has publicly demanded that the Fed cut interest rates. But tariff-driven inflation gives the Fed every reason to keep rates high. The Fed's independence means the President can demand but not compel. The result is a public standoff that further undermines confidence in US institutions.

China Alignment
unintended convergence

China wants a weaker dollar (makes its exports cheaper and erodes US debt in real terms). China wants lower US rates (reduces the yield advantage that attracts capital away from China). The US tariff strategy, through its contradictions, is pushing toward exactly what China wants.

Execution Paradox
the trust deficit

The April 2 tariff announcement was followed by a 90-day pause within days. The pattern — shock announcement, rapid retreat — has been repeated. The world has now priced in a "Trump Risk Premium": US Treasuries trade at higher yields than they otherwise would because investors now treat US policy as less predictable. That premium makes the core fiscal goal more expensive to achieve, not less.

The ironic takeaway

Trump's policies aim to weaken the dollar, cut interest rates, and bring manufacturing home. But the tariff chaos has created a Trump Risk Premium embedded in US Treasury yields — making US borrowing more expensive, strengthening the dollar through safe-haven flows, and raising the cost of the very industrial investment the strategy is meant to attract.

The goals are real. The contradictions are structural. Whether they can be managed without triggering the crisis they're meant to prevent is the open question of the next decade.
