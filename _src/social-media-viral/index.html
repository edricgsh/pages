The video is the cheap part. The first hour is the machine.

Module 05 is where the bootcamp stops making things and starts distributing them — and that's still mostly true of its central claim: reach is an account-hygiene problem and a first-hour problem, not a craft problem. What's changed is how much of the module is now legible. Fifteen of its twenty lessons carry a full transcript, and twelve of those turn out to be step-by-step production workflows — ChatGPT for the idea, Midjourney for the image, Kling AI or Pika for the motion, CapCut for the edit, ElevenLabs or Pixabay for sound — with one creative decision swapped out per niche. Only Lesson 5's branding walkthrough is still video-only.

The big idea

Strip the thirty-two rules down and there are only two moves. The first is keeping the algorithm's model of your account narrow and stable — one niche, one country, one posting slot, one visual look, never a stray video and never a stray login. The second is manufacturing the early-engagement signal in the short window where it decides your reach — planted comments, baited corrections, a full hour of replying to everything that moves.

Note what this implies: the module treats reach as an account-hygiene problem plus a first-hour problem, and that hasn't changed now that the niche pipeline is fully documented. What's new is the layer underneath it — a genuinely repeatable content-production system, the same six tools in the same order twelve times over. Quality was never the hard part here. Distribution was, and now so is supply.

01

The strategy spine — platform, mindset, and the one lesson still on video

Lessons 1–3, 5–6

The module opens with five lessons that set direction: what social media is for, which platform to pick, what mindset and plan to bring, how to brand the channel, and how to actually upload. When this artifact was first written, four of the five existed only as unlisted video links. That's no longer true — Lessons 2, 3 and 6 now carry full Whisper transcripts, and there's real substance in them: a platform comparison, a three-phase mindset framework, and a step-by-step upload walkthrough with a compliance detail (AI-content disclosure) that appears nowhere else in the module. Lesson 5 is the one holdout: still video-only, still the shortest lesson in the module, still the only one hosted on Vimeo instead of Loom.

Lesson 1 is the only written framing in the set, and it's a promise rather than a lesson: you can have the best ads, the best characters or the best visuals — but if nobody sees them, none of it matters. It claims the module will cover hooks that stop the scroll, structuring videos for retention, riding trends without copying, engagement hacks, and making the algorithm push for you. That promise mostly holds up now — the last two land squarely in writing, hooks and retention land partly in the do's list, and "riding trends without copying" turns out, several lessons later, to mean something closer to riding trends by copying (see Do #19).

Choosing A Platformlesson 2 · 3m 26s

TikTok, Instagram or YouTube, compared on one axis: how fast the account compounds. TikTok is the discovery engine — its algorithm is "100% the easiest place to go viral right now," zero-follower accounts have hit ten million views, and the tradeoff is a short shelf life: a video rises fast and, about a week later, "it's almost like the algorithm gets bored of pushing it and stops." Instagram is framed as a personal-brand amplifier — slower to break into, rewarding consistency and aesthetics over raw discovery — but once an account has a following, that following is sticky: a 5k-follower account will "pretty much always hit at least 5k views" per post, because Instagram shows new posts to existing followers by default, which TikTok largely doesn't. YouTube is the long game: harder to go viral, years to build, but a good video "can keep getting views for months or even years," and it's positioned as the platform for real monetisation and authority.

TikTok = fast growth, short memory · Instagram = slow growth, sticky followers · YouTube = slow start, compounds forever

The actual recommendation resolves the three-way choice rather than picking a winner: start on TikTok alone for the first few weeks to get an initial push, then post the same finished video everywhere — Instagram, YouTube, and if there's appetite for it, Facebook and Snapchat too. "You've already made the video. All you need to do is spend an extra 5 minutes posting it to the other platforms." This is where the written Do #18 ("post it everywhere") actually comes from — the video is the source, the PDF just kept the instruction and dropped the platform-by-platform reasoning behind it.

Lesson video

Mindset & Planlesson 3 · 4m 41s

A three-phase framework for what changes about the job as an account grows, and it's the most structurally different piece of writing in the module — nothing else in the written material maps to it directly.

1
Builder

Posting into silence — ten, twenty, fifty videos with barely any traction, and that's normal. The job here is reps, not results: study the platform, shorten content-creation time, build a repeatable workflow, publish on schedule even when nobody's watching, and track process rather than views. "You're not failing. You're learning to create under pressure."

2
Experimenter

Once the workflow is solid, test the actual content: hooks, formats, video length, delivery style, editing pace, thumbnails. This is where analytics start mattering — drop-off points, click-through rate, what gets shared or commented on — and where pattern recognition sets in ("this hook holds people longer," "this format does 3x the others"). The worked example: a "time traveler POV" creator noticed voiceovers increased viewership and let him build storylines with consistent characters. The single biggest mistake named in this phase is not repeating a video that wins — remake it with a small twist, repeat, test.

3
Operator

Traction changes the job again — now it's systems and scale: a content calendar, a standardised workflow, editors or thumbnail designers (or AI automation) to save time, scheduling ahead, and monetisation (affiliate links, digital products, courses, memberships, brand deals). "You're no longer just making videos. You're running a business." The stated discipline at this stage is protecting your energy and delegating: "your job isn't to do every task, it's to make sure the right tasks get done."

The closing lines are the closest thing the module has to a thesis statement: going viral means nothing if you can't repeat it. Consistency beats talent 99% of the time. Most creators fail after they succeed because they didn't build systems. Obsessed in phase one, tactical in phase two, strategic in phase three.

02

Nineteen do's, regrouped into the five jobs they actually do

Lesson 4 — AVB TikTok Secrets · now on video too

The source is a flat numbered list. Flat is how it was written, not how it works: the nineteen items are doing five distinct jobs, and reading them grouped makes the logic visible — most of the list is about giving the algorithm a clean, repeatable signal, not about making the video better. Original numbering is preserved so each item maps back. Lesson 4's video, What Makes A Video Viral?, now has a transcript, and it tracks the PDF almost sentence for sentence — the presenter narrates the same nineteen items in the same order, calling the source document "gold dust" and pointing viewers to it in the swipe file section of the course. The one thing the video adds that the PDF doesn't is tone: it closes with "collected by years of experience in the game — take this information in and apply it as soon as possible," a stronger claim of first-hand authority than the written page makes on its own.

Cadence — make the variable you're testing the only variable

1
Post at the same time every day

Find your slot, then never move it. The stated reason is measurement, not the algorithm: if posting time floats, you can't tell whether a weak video was weak or just badly timed. Hold it constant and the metrics become readable.

4
Three to five posts a day, maximum

A ceiling, not a target. Paired with Don't #5 — at least one to two hours between posts — it defines the whole posting rhythm.

13
Ride your own momentum

When a video is performing, do not go quiet. Post again soon, because the people currently watching the winner will be shown the follow-up. Waiting days to capitalise wastes the audience the hit just bought you.

Retention — the only metric that is said to matter

17
First clip: three seconds, hard limit

The most concrete craft rule in the module. If the viewer has to wait five seconds for a scene change, they scroll. The hook isn't a line of copy here — it's a cut.

10
Put text on screen

The reasoning is mechanical rather than aesthetic: if people are reading, that means they are still watching. Text buys watch time directly.

6
Track watch time, and act on the drop-off point

Watch time is called the single most crucial factor. The instruction is specific: if viewers are leaving at two seconds, change the next video — not the caption, not the hashtags, and not this video.

12
Keep the thumbnails consistent

A profile grid that looks like one series converts a single-video visitor into a follower. This is the retention rule that operates above the video, at the page level.

Manufactured engagement — the comment count is the lever

2
Comment bait: plant something wrong

Put something annoying, random, or flatly out of place in the video. People will point it out. Every correction is a comment, and comments push the video further. Expanded into a full tactic in section 04.

7
Build a comment pod

Make friends on the platform and message each other on every post. Ten reciprocating creators is ten comments the instant you publish — the same effect as the seeded-comment hack, sourced from real accounts.

16
Reply to every comment — and reply with a question

Not "thanks". A question, because a question turns one comment into a thread. See Don't #9: emoji-only replies are counted as weaker than full sentences.

3
Put a follower goal in the bio

6500/7000 🎯. The mechanic is participation — people follow because it feels like moving a number, not like doing you a favour.

8
Screenshot every post into your story

Free reach from the audience you already have, on a surface that competes with nothing.

Teaching the algorithm who you are

11
Watch your own niche, 10–15 minutes a day

Like, comment and watch inside your niche daily. Your consumption is how the platform decides whose feed your content belongs in. Don't #4 is the strict version: never watch anything else from that account.

9
Four hashtags, no more

Beyond four, the stated failure mode is that the algorithm gets confused about what the video is. Hashtags here are classification, not discovery.

5
Target a country through the content itself

Want a US audience? Make US-flavoured content and tag #USA, #Newyork. This matters commercially — RPM varies enormously by country — and it is the flip side of the geography rules in Don'ts #8 and #12.

14
Short, precise bio with emoji

The bio is read by a human deciding whether to follow after one video. It has one job and about two seconds to do it.

Supply — never run out of the next video

19
Recreate what worked yesterday, today

The bluntest line in the document: if someone in your niche posted a video yesterday and it has two million views, remake it with small changes and post it now. Do not try to re-invent the wheel. Note the tension with Lesson 1's promise to "ride trends without copying" — the written material does not honour that distinction.

15
Bookmark relentlessly

Save ideas as you scroll so the empty-tank day never happens. The bookmarks folder is the content pipeline.

18
Post it everywhere

The hard part — making the video — is already done. A few extra minutes on YouTube and Instagram doubles or triples reach. The only argument in the module against single-platform focus.

03

Thirteen ways to get throttled

Lesson 4 — AVB TikTok Secrets · now on video too

The do's are optimisations; the don'ts are damage. Almost all of them share one root cause — you gave the platform contradictory evidence about who you are, and it rebuilt its model of your account around the contradiction. The four costliest are broken out first because they are hard or impossible to undo. The transcript of Lesson 4 covers all thirteen in the same order as the PDF, word for word in most places — the only place it visibly diverges is pacing, where a couple of items get repeated mid-sentence as the presenter re-reads the slide.

8
Never take the account abroad

UK creator with a 95% UK audience flies to Thailand, opens the app, and TikTok now believes the account is Thai — and pushes the videos to a Thai audience. The document's assessment is unusually stark: you may never recover from this. The same mechanism, deliberately triggered, is Don't #1: logging into someone else's account from your phone is described as an instant IP ban.

2
Never post on a cold account

Warm it up for at least seven days by watching content in your niche. Two stated tests for readiness: follow someone and check that your own follower count moves when you return to your profile; and post one throwaway video — if it gets zero views, the account is not ready.

11
Never post off-niche — not even once

Stated with no exceptions. One stray video re-opens a classification question you spent weeks closing. Don't #4 extends it to consumption: if you want to watch things outside the niche, use a different account entirely.

6
Never use Promote

The claim is that paying once teaches the platform you are willing to pay, and it throttles the organic reach of everything you don't promote from then on. Treat this as the document's assertion rather than a verified platform behaviour — but the cost of being wrong is asymmetric.

Location, language, niche, watch history and posting rhythm are all inputs to the same model, and the module's position is that contradicting it is far more expensive than any single bad video.
04

Four engagement cheat codes

Lesson 4 · Lesson 20 — AVB TikTok Engagement Hacks

The second cheat-sheet, and the sharpest thinking in the module — and the one document in Section 02–04 with no video attached to it anywhere. Lesson 4's transcript covers the do's and don'ts from Secrets in full but never touches the four codes below; there is no spoken version of this material in either lesson it's attached to. All four exploit the same observed window: the first one to two hours decide the video's reach, and within that window comment volume is the strongest signal you can influence for free. The underlying psychology is stated plainly — most people don't want to be the first to comment. If the section already looks alive, they join. If it looks empty, they scroll.

Code 1 · Filter the comment you don't want, keep the engagementhidden but counted

Use on any content where a predictable objection breaks the illusion — for AI video, the person who zooms in and posts "this is AI", after which everyone else piles on.

Settings and Privacy → Comments → Filter keywords → add the phrases your audience repeats, especially the negative ones

The mechanic that makes this more than moderation: filtered comments still count toward engagement. They are hidden from public view unless you approve them. So the fiftieth person to think they are the first to expose you comments anyway — unaware the previous forty-nine were filtered — and the algorithm reads every one of them as activity while the visible comment section stays on-message.

Code 2 · Seed the argument from a second devicemanufactured momentum

Use in the 5–10 minutes after posting, before the real audience arrives.

post → wait 5–10 min → comment from a second device asking a divisive, opinion-based question → reply from the main account and argue → keep both sides going as real people join

Not a generic "great video" — the planted comment has to create a divide and make people take a side. The stated result is five to ten comments within minutes, which is enough for the section to read as an existing discussion rather than an empty one. The operational footnote: use mobile data rather than the same WiFi, and an account not linked to your main one.

The claim attached is specific — this is the difference between a video that caps at 300 views and one that clears 10k.

Code 3 · Work the first sixty minutes like a job1 comment → 3–4

Every post, immediately after Code 2. Set an actual timer.

reply instantly to everything, even "lol" → never say "thanks" → ask a follow-up, add a twist, make a joke → turn each comment into a micro-conversation → engage the negative ones hardest

The multiplier is the point: twenty people commenting can show a count of seventy if you worked the section properly, and the algorithm cannot tell the difference between a thread you built and a thread that happened. Hostile comments are treated as the best material — go back and forth respectfully, and others join in.

Code 4 · Plant a mistake worth correctingego as engagement

Strongest on educational, historical, storytelling, AI and "did you know" content — anywhere the viewer can feel clever for catching you.

post as normal → hide one believable error in the text, speech or captions → let the corrections roll in → reply vaguely ("Are you sure?") to keep the loop open

Worked examples from the document: a Nike logo on a shoe in a 1950s scene, "Einstein invented gravity", the internet dated to 1995, Jupiter labelled Saturn, a giraffe in the background for a split second, your for you're in a caption. Most people scroll past without commenting; the instant they spot something wrong, the reflex to correct overrides that.

The calibration rule is the whole craft here: the mistake has to be believable. Too obvious and it reads as bait, which costs you more than the comments are worth. The goal is to just barely trigger the correction.

Read the fine print before running any of these. Codes 2 and 3 depend on operating a second account you control and disguising the connection — that is coordinated inauthentic behaviour under TikTok's own rules, not a grey area, and detection costs the account the module spent seven days warming up. Code 4 means deliberately publishing false statements to an audience, which has a reputational cost the document doesn't price. Code 1 is the mildest, but filtering "this is AI" is specifically suppressing disclosure of synthetic media — a position that is getting harder to hold now that Lesson 6 has creators ticking an AI-disclosure box on every upload. None of this is an argument that they don't work; it's the cost side, which the source leaves out entirely.
05

The twelve niche formats — the pipeline, the tools, and what actually changes per niche

Lessons 7–19 · prompt pack + 12 workflow transcripts

Twelve consecutive lessons, one per proven format, about 45 minutes of video between them. When this page was first written, none of them had a transcript, so the only way to reconstruct what each niche actually was came from Lesson 7's prompt pack — fifty-odd Midjourney prompts with no process attached. All twelve niche lessons are transcribed now, and it turns out the process was the content. Read the twelve back to back and they collapse into one workflow — ChatGPT, Midjourney, Kling AI, CapCut, ElevenLabs — repeated with one creative decision changed each time. The prompt pack wasn't the format. It was step three of six.

Two of the twelve were misread from the prompts alone, and the transcripts correct both. "Animal Hybrid Fusion" looked like an unresolved standoff between two animals — a matchup the video never settles. It isn't. The standoff is the opening frame of a Kling keyframe morph that ends with an actual hybrid on screen (a lion rendered with crocodile scales, in the source example). "Knights As Emotions" undersold its own range — the lesson's own framing is "you can do this niche for anything you want," with emotions used only as the demonstration. The one label that turns out exactly right is "Animals," billed Animals Helping in the pack: an animal warns a vehicle away from another animal, no narration required, no correction needed.

The standard pipeline — what most of the twelve niches actually share

Strip the animal names and the armour colours away and almost every niche lesson teaches the same seven-step loop. The differences that remain are small and specific enough to state per niche rather than repeat in every entry below.

1
Capture a reference

Snipping Tool on Windows (or Screen Grab on Mac) pulls a still frame straight off an existing viral video in the niche — the character, the object, the exact truck. Most niches start here rather than from a blank prompt, because matching an existing channel's look is treated as the fast path to a proven format, not a shortcut around originality.

2
ChatGPT for ideas and prompts

A fixed meta-prompt — sometimes "give me ten video ideas for this niche," sometimes "write Midjourney prompts based on this screenshot" — does the brainstorming so the creator never has to invent a scene from nothing. Every niche lesson routes through ChatGPT at least once before Midjourney ever opens.

3
Midjourney for the image

The ChatGPT-written prompt goes into Midjourney, often with the Step 1 screenshot attached as a character or style reference so the render locks onto the source. Generate, pick the best, upscale.

4
Kling AI for the motion

The Midjourney image goes into Kling, with a short prompt describing what should happen in the shot. Output is usually a five-second clip, and it comes out watermarked unless downloaded through the correct button.

5
CapCut for the edit

Clips get dragged in, text and transitions get added, multiple generated shots get stitched into one video. Several lessons point back to "the CapCut masterclass" from earlier in the course rather than re-explain the editor from scratch.

6
Sound

ElevenLabs for generated sound effects (prompt influence 80, duration capped at 5 seconds so it doesn't overrun the clip) or character voiceover, Pixabay for free stock effects and trending audio. A couple of niches skip effects entirely and just lay down music.

7
Export and upload

1080p, 30fps, downloaded without watermark, uploaded to TikTok first. The same settings sheet from Section 02, applied identically across every niche.

Three niches genuinely leave this loop: Evolutions replaces Kling with Pika's two-image keyframe interpolation and chains it across a whole life story; Animal Hybrid Fusion uses Kling's start-frame/end-frame mode to morph one image into another instead of animating a single image; Animal Fusion keeps the standard loop but bolts a split-screen "versus" edit onto the front of it in CapCut. Everything below is the seven steps with the details that actually vary.

01 · Evolutionslesson 8 · 5m 11s · keyframe chain

Celebrity life-story videos, cool transitions carrying one face through decades — the reference channel gets millions of views per video doing exactly this. It was the one niche with nothing recoverable from the prompt pack; the transcript fills the whole gap.

ChatGPT (scene list + MJ prompts) → Google (real reference photo per era) → colourise if needed → Midjourney with photo as character reference → Pika keyframes (photo A → photo B) → CapCut

ChatGPT gets one prompt — celebrity name, number of life-stage scenes — and returns both the scene breakdown and the Midjourney prompt for each one. For every scene, the actual production step is research: Google the celebrity's name plus that scene's time period to find a real photo, colourise it if it's not already in colour, then feed it into Midjourney as a character reference alongside the ChatGPT prompt, so the render is recognisably that person rather than a generic face. Adjacent scenes that look similar can share one reference photo (the transcript's example: 1884 and 1887 use the same source image).

The transition itself isn't a Kling job — it's Pika's keyframe mode, which takes two images and interpolates a smooth morph between them at a 9:16 aspect ratio. The continuity trick that makes a multi-scene video read as one unbroken transformation: the last frame used in transition N becomes the first frame of transition N+1. A paid CapCut plan is required to export without a watermark.

Lesson video

02 · POVlesson 9 · 7m 02s · immersion

First-person perspective dropped into a loaded moment: Omaha Beach, an Egyptian pyramid ramp, a Puritan witch trial, a 1980s basement with an NES controller, the Apollo 11 cockpit. The longest niche video in the module, and the one with the most moving production parts.

ChatGPT (10 ideas) → ChatGPT (per-scene breakdown, TikTok-optimised) → Midjourney with a detailed prompt, not a plain one → Kling AI → CapCut text + layered ElevenLabs SFX → black-fade transitions

ChatGPT does the idea generation twice — once for ten video concepts, once to break the chosen concept ("the Black Plague," in the walkthrough) into per-scene descriptions already optimised for TikTok. The lesson makes a point of showing why the second ChatGPT pass matters: typing "wake up in a grimy medieval alleyway" straight into Midjourney just renders an alleyway, no waking-up implied. The ChatGPT-expanded version — spelling out the visible hands, the specific props, the sensory detail — is what actually produces a POV shot rather than a wide shot. The visible hands are the load-bearing element in every prompt in the pack; strip them out and the format reverts to an ordinary establishing shot.

Sound gets more attention here than in any other niche: ElevenLabs' sound-effects tab, prompt influence set to 80 and duration capped at 5 seconds, multiple layered effects per scene rather than one (a "busy medieval mood" track under the main effect, in the worked example), volumes adjusted per layer, and black-fade transitions between scenes with adjustable duration. If nothing comes to mind, ChatGPT can be handed the Midjourney image directly and asked for sound-effect suggestions to match it.

03 · Animal Hybrid Fusionlesson 10 · 2m 43s · keyframe morph

Two real animals shown together, then morphed into one hybrid creature on screen — the source example, a crocodile and a lion, sits at 44 million views on the reference channel. This is the niche the prompt pack's "standoff" images belong to, and the transcript is what reveals they were never meant to stand still.

screenshot two animals together → ChatGPT → Midjourney (pair image) → ChatGPT again (describe the hybrid) → Midjourney (hybrid image) → Kling 1.6, pair image as start frame, hybrid image as end frame → morph video

Stage one produces exactly the images the prompt pack shows — two animals side by side on a shared riverbank or canyon, described in the pack as if the tension between them were the whole point. It isn't; it's the start frame. Stage two asks ChatGPT for a description of what the two animals fuse into (the worked example: "a lion, but with crocodile scales"), renders that in Midjourney, and then both images go into Kling 1.6 specifically — the pair as the start frame, the hybrid as the end frame — with a prompt describing the animals coming together and merging. The output is a single continuous shot of two animals fusing into one. The whole loop, per the lesson, takes under an hour for several videos.

04 · Animal Fusionlesson 11 · 4m 21s · split-screen fusion

An animal fused with an object — the worked example takes an ostrich and honey and merges them into one liquid-gold creature. Not, as the prompt pack alone suggested, a two-stage "boring animal, then material payoff" prompt — the pack's Liquid honey / Liquid gold lines are the fusion ingredient, not a plot twist inside a single prompt.

ChatGPT (10 animal+object pairings) → Midjourney: animal ("full body") + Midjourney: material, each saved as a character reference → combine with one prompt → Kling AI → CapCut split-screen intro → fused video

ChatGPT supplies ten animal-and-object pairings so the creative decision is picking, not inventing. The animal gets its own simple Midjourney prompt — "full body" is called out specifically, so the whole creature is in frame rather than a crop — and the object or material gets a separate, equally simple prompt. Both saved images are then dragged into Midjourney's prompt bar as character references and combined with one more short prompt describing the fusion; a location can be added for specificity (the lesson's example: putting the fused ostrich "in the desert").

The CapCut step is what makes this niche visually distinct from the rest of the pipeline: the two original, unfused source images — animal alone, material alone — are masked into a split screen down the centre (mask → rectangle, resized to fill each half), a cross-shaped sticker is dropped over the seam, and a transition effect plays across the boundary before cutting to the fused Kling video. The split screen is the reveal; the fusion clip is the payoff.

06 · Tim Cheeselesson 13 · 4m 52s · recurring character

Anthropomorphic animals in mafia-noir wardrobe, with an actual ongoing plot — "probably the most popular AI niche right now," per the lesson, and the storylines are open enough that the audience keeps adding characters to the lore. This is the most production-heavy niche in the module by a clear margin: it's the only one that scripts dialogue and casts two distinct voices.

ChatGPT (idea + full script) → screenshot the character from an existing video → ChatGPT (place that exact character in a new scene) → Midjourney, 9:16 enforced → Kling AI per shot → ElevenLabs (two voices, reading the script) + Pixabay SFX → CapCut auto-captions + transitions + trending track

ChatGPT writes the concept and the script in one pass — usable as-is or as a base to punch up. The character itself comes from a screenshot of an existing Tim Cheese video, fed to ChatGPT with a prompt instructing it to replicate that character into any new environment (the worked example is a bank-robbery scene); ChatGPT then generates a run of per-scene Midjourney prompts designed to flow as one storyline, with 9:16 enforced explicitly — if Midjourney drifts off it, the fix is to tell ChatGPT to correct the aspect ratio in the next prompt. A second character (the betrayer, "John Pork," in the example) is added the same way: a short description to ChatGPT, additional angles generated on request.

Each image becomes a clip in Kling, with room for creative camera moves. Then two ElevenLabs voices read the ChatGPT script — one per character — and Pixabay supplies extra effects (a gunshot, in the example). CapCut assembles it with auto-generated, styled captions, transitions between clips, and layered sound (the walkthrough stacks a motorcycle effect, a blowtorch and a gunshot), plus the niche's own recognisable backing track, either added in the edit directly or picked from TikTok's sound library at upload time — "the most popular song that is used on most of this niche's videos." The finished sample line gives the tone away: "I thought we were partners... Sorry Tim, this bacon's going solo."

The prompt pack — the grammar, and six verbatim templates

Lesson 7 — What's this for?

Lesson 7 has a title that tells you nothing, no video, and it's still the most valuable written asset in the module: roughly fifty verbatim image prompts across eleven of the twelve niches. Read against the workflow in Section 05, it's clear what these actually are — the output of step 2 of the standard pipeline (ChatGPT turning an idea into a prompt) and the input to step 3 (Midjourney turning the prompt into an image). The pack skips both ends of that: no ChatGPT reasoning behind why each prompt is shaped the way it is, and no indication of what happens to the image afterward in Kling or Pika. What's left is the grammar — genuinely useful on its own, because eleven niches' worth of prompts converge on the same six-slot sentence structure whether or not you ever watch a video. It opens with its own instruction for how to use it, worth quoting exactly because it reframes the pack from fifty prompts into a reusable method:

ALL PROMPTS ARE ADJUSTABLE AND CAN BE APPLIED TO ANY NICHE,
JUST ASK CHATGPT TO 'COPY THIS STYLE OF PROMPT BUT CHANGE X'
(EXAMPLES: MAKE CHARACTER SOMEWHERE ELSE, MAKE CHARACTER LOOK
DIFFERENT ETC)

The six slots every prompt in the pack fills

Read all fifty and one sentence structure repeats almost without variation. It is not written down anywhere in the course, but it is the actual template:

1  REALISM CLAIM   "a hyper-realistic scene of…" / "a full body shot of…"
2  SUBJECT         the creature, character or object, named specifically
3  WARDROBE        clothing, armour, anatomy, held props — the identity layer
4  SETTING         one location, stated concretely, never abstract
5  ATMOSPHERE      light source, weather, time of day, what's in the background
6  TONE + TAIL     the genre in three words, then "cinematic lighting, 4K detail"

Two things are consistently absent, and both are deliberate. There is no camera movement — these are image prompts, and motion comes later from the video model. And there is no story: every prompt describes a single frozen moment, because the format's job is to make the first three seconds unskippable, not to narrate.

The recurring tail phrases

The last clause of nearly every prompt is assembled from this bank. They function as quality and genre switches rather than description.

4K detail ultra-realism hyper-realistic cinematic lighting dramatic shadows ultra-detailed textures shallow depth of field nostalgic 35mm film grain gritty cartoon realism 4K cinematic cartoon realism meme-style character design fantasy absurdism creature discovery energy villain reveal energy deep-sea horror meets industrial realism underdog training montage energy whimsical yet believable wildlife photography style noir atmosphere dark fairytale meets action-thriller

The transferable lesson isn't the prompts, and it isn't just the fixed-sentence-one-variable trick either. It's steps 1–2 of Section 05's pipeline done well: hand ChatGPT a style you like and ask it to substitute one variable, and it will keep producing prompts in this exact grammar indefinitely. That's what makes a niche postable three to five times a day for months without running dry — Do #4 and Do #15's supply problem, solved upstream of Midjourney entirely. Pick the format by how many times you can turn its variable, not by how good the first render looks.

The working sequence. Warm the account for seven days inside one niche. Pick a format whose single variable you can turn a hundred times — ChatGPT for the idea, Midjourney for the image, Kling or Pika for the motion, CapCut for the cut, ElevenLabs or Pixabay for the sound. Cut the first shot under three seconds and put text on screen. Export 1080p at 30fps with the AI-disclosure toggle ticked, four hashtags, same slot every day, three to five posts with two hours between them. Then spend the first hour replying to everything, because that hour is the only part of the process where your effort still changes the outcome. And never, ever open the app abroad.
