1. Pushing Software Engineering Limits with "Napkin Math" Jul 21

Simon Eskildsen (turbopuffer) on first-principles engineering
Jul 21, 2026
Read original →

The core concept: "Napkin math" — computing theoretical lower bounds for system performance using first-principles calculations. Simon maintains a table of ~50 hardware/cost numbers memorized via flashcards, then sanity-checks everything against these limits.

Key Takeaways

  • Toxiproxy origins: Simon built Shopify's Toxiproxy (failure-injection tool, now OSS) after a cascading Redis failure during Black Friday. It's used by 37% of the Fortune 100.
  • How turbopuffer started: Summer 2023. Simon realized existing vector search was 10–20× more expensive than napkin math suggested. First version: cluster vectors in S3 + NGINX caching.
  • Cursor as customer #1: Simon tweeted about the product, flew to SF, debugged Cursor's Postgres (autovacuum!), promised 95% cost reduction — delivered ($80K → $4K/month).
  • VC funding critique: 6 reasons to raise capital (R&D, growth, ego, employee rewards, partnerships, M&A). "Massaging founder egos" is dangerously common.
  • Insane efficiency: Turbopuffer crossed $100M ARR with less than $1M total raised — virtually unheard of in modern SaaS.
"I learned that you must avoid digging holes. Repeatedly, I found myself so fixated on getting a particular idea to work that I'd get absolutely nowhere. Sometimes, you have to bite the bullet, delete your program, find a new sheet of paper, and start from scratch."

3. Slow Down to Speed Up: AI Agents & Code Quality Jun 2

The paradox of AI-generated code volume
Jun 2, 2026
Read original →

The thesis: AI coding tools are now used by virtually all engineers. Code output has doubled (or more) in just 6 months. This creates a crisis of quality, security, and tech debt that nobody is managing.

The Downstream Problems

  • Quality — code volume outpaces verification capacity
  • Security — AI-generated code introduces vulnerabilities that aren't caught
  • Infra strain — CI/CD pipelines, review processes, and testing can't keep up
  • Debt — unverified code merges compound

The rational fix: Cap AI-generated code output to what teams can actually verify and review. Slow down the rate to a pace where humans can maintain quality and reliability.

The paradox: While this is the rational solution, most teams and companies are not acting rationally. They're prioritizing speed over quality. Cursor power users generate 10× more code than median users. Nearly half of AI-generated changes are accepted without manual review.

"Devs are generating twice as much code (or more) than just 6 months ago, which is a problem for quality, reliability, and tech debt. A rational fix is available — but who's acting rationally?"

4. AI's Impact on Software Engineers in 2026, Part 2 May 19

900+ survey: the good, the bad, and the rollbacks
May 19, 2026
Read original →

Key Findings from 900+ Respondents

  • AI amplifies culture — it doesn't fix underlying quality issues. Teams with strong engineering practices get more benefit.
  • Rollback cases — at least one 10,000+ person European company rolled back AI tools after costly production incidents caused by AI-written code.
  • Codebase quality is declining — "AI slop" (duplicated, verbose code), too many reviews → review quality slips, more bugs.
  • Junior engineers suffer — they're the top token spenders in orgs but get fewer growth opportunities as seniors delegate to AI instead.
  • "Death of code review" — reviewers lack motivation to deeply review giant AI-generated PRs.
  • "Tokenmaxxing" — companies pushing for universal AI usage targets (50%, 80%, 100%) without regard for quality.
"Since the AI boom, the quality of technical writing and reasoning from senior engineers in my org has significantly deteriorated. We've also seen costly production incidents caused by code written and/or approved by AI, and — while my employer initially bought heavily into the hype — we have now rolled back some of our AI tools."
— Engineering lead, 10,000+ person company, Europe
"For junior engineers like myself, these AI tools are stressful to use. I don't have the experience or knowledge to tell AI exactly what to do or quickly confirm its output, so I spend a lot of time on just triple checking and redoing stuff."
— Junior engineer, Australia

5. Job Market 2026, Part 2: AI Labs, Frontend Decline & Comp Jun 9

Anthropic > Big Tech, the end of frontend-only roles, and the "great flattening"
Jun 9, 2026
Read original →

Anthropic Is Now the #1 Most In-Demand Employer

Combined, Anthropic + OpenAI account for 51% of all interviewing.io coaching requests — more than Google, Meta, Apple, and Microsoft combined. OpenAI (16%) gets about the same candidate share as Google (17%).

Frontend-Only Engineers Are Disappearing

Full-stack is becoming the norm. With AI removing barriers between frontend and backend work, "pure" frontend engineers will only be employed at larger companies with dedicated design system teams. Native iOS/Android roles are also in structural decline.

New Grad & Intern Hiring Is Still Falling

This cycle is historically unprecedented: even as overall SWE hiring recovers, intern programs keep shrinking. Large tech companies take on half as many interns as before. In 2025, just 1 in 10 engineering hires at larger companies were recent grads, down from nearly 3 in 10 in 2023.

Cross-Cutting Themes Synthesis

📊 The AI Divide Is Real

The job market is bifurcating. AI engineering hiring grew 60% YoY vs 7% for SWE. AI compensation already exceeds pure SWE. Meanwhile, frontend, mobile, and generalist roles face structural compression. The gap isn't cyclical — it's permanent.

⚠️ Quality Is the New Bottleneck

Across three separate articles, the same pattern emerges: AI lets engineers produce code 2× faster, but the surrounding infrastructure (code review, testing, security review, maintenance) hasn't scaled. The result is "AI slop," production incidents, and a maintenance burden falling on fewer people. The rational fix — slowing down — is politically difficult when management is measuring velocity.

🔬 First Principles Still Win

Simon Eskildsen's turbopuffer story is almost a counterpoint to the AI hype: sometimes the biggest competitive advantage comes from understanding your hardware costs deeply enough to realize everyone else is overpaying by 10–20×. Napkin math + focus beats throwing models at a problem.

🎯 The Junior Engineer Trap

Multiple data points converge: juniors spend the most on AI tokens but get the least value. Seniors delegate fewer growth opportunities to them (using AI instead). New grad hiring is structurally declining. The industry is eating its seed corn.

🏢 The "Great Flattening" of Engineering Management

Fewer engineering managers per engineer. Fewer VP/Director posts. AI labs have radically flat hierarchies. The traditional career ladder (IC → Manager → Director → VP) is compressing. Staff+ roles are simultaneously the hottest and hardest to land.

Bottom Line

The Pragmatic Engineer's last five editions paint a picture of an industry in transition: AI is real, it's reshaping both the job market and the daily work of engineering, but the side effects (quality erosion, juniors left behind, maintenance crisis, management flattening) are accumulating faster than most companies are addressing them. The winners will be those who pair AI acceleration with disciplined verification — not those who optimize for output alone.
